A Thousand or Fifty Thousand Years in the Quran?

The verses describe different settings. Clock experiments measure relativity; they do not set a duration for unseen days.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 28 September 2026English edition updated: 14 October 2026Independent physical and exegetical review: not yet completed

Short answer

The Quran does not lay down a rule that every day “with God” equals one thousand years and then contradict it with fifty thousand. The numbers occur in different settings: people impatient for punishment in Quran 22:47; the governance and ascent of the command in 32:5; and the ascent of the angels and Spirit in 70:4, which some commentators also connect to the Day of Resurrection. Exegetes differ about what each “day” denotes. Joining all three into a single equation to allege contradiction or compute the speed of a celestial being ignores that context. Relativity experimentally establishes that material clocks can tick at different rates with motion and gravity. That fact cannot tell us the duration of an unseen day or interpret these verses through Einstein's equations.

Why not simply calculate with 1,000 and 50,000?

If one journey takes an hour and another five hours, there is no contradiction until we know that they are the same journey, along the same route and in the same conditions. In these verses we must first ask which event is in view, whom the address concerns and whether a number gives a travel measure, describes an event of the Hereafter or places human impatience in perspective.

This is the question behind pages 29–31 of Qul Siru fi al-Ard. The book puts the thousand and fifty thousand beside the hadith of a day like a year, and mentions relativity with a qualification: different measures of “day” in scripture neither explain physics equations nor provide a conversion table for the days of creation. Here each text is examined in its own setting before any comparison.

Three passages, three questions

Passage Statement in context Question
Quran 22:47 “They urge you to hasten the punishment, but God will never break His promise. A day with your Lord is like a thousand years of what you count.” Why does a delayed promise seem to impatient people like a broken one?
Quran 32:5 “He governs the command from heaven to earth; then it ascends to Him in a day whose measure is a thousand years of what you count.” What is the ascent of the command and the measure of its day?
Quran 70:4 “The angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years.” Which ascent or day does this measure describe?

The first passage uses a comparison, “like a thousand years,” within an answer to demands for punishment. The other two speak of a “measure” together with ascent. That distinction does not settle every interpretive dispute, but it prevents us from deleting the verbs and contexts and keeping only two bare numbers.

Quran 22:47: a divine clock?

The passage begins “They seek to hasten you with the punishment.” Al-Tabari records interpretations connecting the “day” with creation, the Hereafter or the meaning of forbearance and weight. In the immediate setting he favors a response to human haste: what people think delayed does not escape God's appointed time, as the surrounding verses continue with the respite given to unjust towns. This is a reading of an admonition, not a measurement of a “clock with God” against an Earth clock.1

The phrase “with your Lord” does not license placing the Creator at a high elevation in the universe, estimating a gravitational potential and assigning Him an atomic clock. Physics compares physical clocks along specified paths and in specified places. The verse addresses God's knowledge, promise and determination in a warning. Moving a model from the observable world to the unseen needs evidence beyond a shared word for “time.”

Quran 32:5: a thousand years for the command's ascent

The verse starts with a verb that should not be removed: God “governs the command,” and then the command “ascends to Him.” Al-Tabari records an explanation of the thousand as the measure of a descent and ascent by human reckoning, another relating it to a day of creation, and other readings of the command or angels. These are attempts to understand a text concerning the unseen. Distances in some transmitted reports cannot simply be turned into modern astronomical measurements.2

The same number in Quran 22:47 and 32:5 does not establish that the verses describe one physical day. Even a commentator who relates the passages must still examine 70:4 in its own context. Dividing fifty thousand by one thousand produces no “time dilation factor” unless observers, clocks, paths and events are defined.

Quran 70:4: why fifty thousand years?

Al-Tabari first treats the verse as referring to the ascent of the angels and Spirit over a distance distinct from that in 32:5. He also reports a view that it concerns the Day of Resurrection and its length or severity for those facing judgment. This diversity is old. It would be misleading to say all exegetes chose the Resurrection, or that a reported distance is an astronomical fact observable with a telescope.3

Al-Tabari also transmits a response attributed to Ibn Abbas: when asked about the thousand-year day, he asked in turn about the fifty-thousand-year day and declined to speak beyond what he knew, saying that God knows them. However one evaluates a preferred interpretation, the report models caution. A number in scripture does not require us to possess a conversion formula for every unseen event.4

Thus the alleged contradiction between “a thousand” and “fifty thousand” requires proof that the passages give different quantities for the same event in the same respect. Their distinct settings and recorded interpretive alternatives make that premise unjustified. This textual answer does not need to recruit relativity whenever two numbers differ.

The day like a year: “estimate its measure”

Muslim reports from Al-Nawwas ibn Sam’an, may God be pleased with him, concerning the Dajjal: one of his days is like a year, another like a month, another like a week, and the rest like people’s usual days. When the companions asked whether a single day’s prayers would suffice for the day like a year, the answer was: “No, estimate its measure.”5

The question and answer address worship and prayer times when a familiar temporal pattern no longer applies. The report does not state that the Dajjal moves near light speed, explain a physical cause for the long day or describe the six days of creation. The book uses it to show varied usage of “day” and the need for estimation, not as an astronomy experiment.

What does relativity actually measure?

In special relativity, a clock carried along one path can record a different elapsed time from a comparison made in a suitable reference frame. For uniform motion in an inertial frame, a simple form is:

Δτ = Δt √(1 − v²/c²)

Here Δτ is proper time registered by the traveling clock; v is speed relative to the chosen frame; and c is light speed. The formula says nothing about a fixed number of years for “a day with God.” It requires a physical clock, path, reference frame and motion.

General relativity also predicts different clock rates at different gravitational potentials. For two stationary clocks separated by a small height h near Earth's surface, an approximate fractional frequency difference is:

Δf/f ≈ gh/c²

Researchers at the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology raised one of two atomic clocks by about 33 centimeters and measured a difference consistent with the higher clock ticking slightly faster. They also measured a speed-related clock effect. At that height the approximation gives about 3.6×10⁻¹⁷, nowhere near turning a human day into a thousand years, let alone fifty thousand.67

This does not mean that scripture is “wrong because it does not match the experiment.” The questions differ. The experiment compares material clocks under defined conditions; interpretation asks what a discourse about warning, governance, ascent and the Hereafter means. An equation cannot select one of al-Tabari's readings, and a pair of scriptural numbers cannot supply unspecified speeds or reference frames for an equation.

Where do the text and science meet, and where not?

A modest point of contact is sound: neither language nor physics requires every reference to “a day” to denote one 24-hour terrestrial rotation. But a verbal resemblance involving different measures of time does not mean that the causes are the same, or that a scientific theory can be deduced from these passages.

A claim that these verses anticipated relativity would require something more precise: a comparison of clocks associated with differently moving observers or different gravitational potentials, and a quantitative relation testable without selecting a meaning only after learning the theory. The verses do not specify those things. Computing an angel's velocity from a supposed distance and a thousand years adds an observer, clock, path and distance that the text does not give.

Likewise, relativity is no reason to reject the verses. They do not state that every physical day has an identical duration and then assign inconsistent values. Interpretation of a religious text, measurement of nature and a believer's reflection on creation are different activities with different evidential limits.

What about the six days in the book?

The book does not claim that six days of creation equal six thousand years by multiplying six by Quran 22:47, or three hundred thousand years by multiplying by 70:4. On pages 28–31 it affirms the six days while leaving their measure and manner without a conclusive scientific timetable. It distinguishes broadening our concept of time from converting scripture into Einstein's equations.

A separate Arabic article on the six days examines the wording, interpretive dispute and the universe's age. This study asks the narrower question: why does one passage say a thousand and another fifty thousand, and what can and cannot be learned by comparing that question with relativity?

The book page collects the broader path from creation and cosmic time to the limits of knowledge. The book frames the inquiry; the independent exegetical and experimental sources for this article follow below.

Conclusion

The thousand and fifty thousand are not contradictory settings of a “divine clock.” Each verse has its own setting, and classical interpretations of the days must be represented fairly. Relativity is well tested with atomic clocks, but it does not turn the days of al-Hajj, al-Sajda or al-Ma'arij into speed and gravity equations. Careful reading protects the text from a question it did not pose and science from claims its tools cannot support. It also leaves room for the restraint attributed to Ibn Abbas in speaking of what one does not know.

Frequently asked questions

Is a day with God a thousand or fifty thousand years?

These passages do not give one conversion law for every day. A thousand occurs in a warning against hastening punishment and in the governance and ascent of the command; fifty thousand occurs in the ascent of the angels and Spirit, with interpretations also relating it to the Resurrection. The subject of each verse must be identified before comparison.

Do Quran 32:5 and 70:4 contradict each other?

That would require proof that both describe the same event from the same perspective while assigning incompatible measures. Commentators transmitted different views of the ascent of the command, the angels and the Day of Resurrection. The required identity has not been established.

Does the Quran state the theory of relativity?

The term “day” can be broader than the usual Earth day, which may prompt reflection. But the verses do not specify reference frames, velocity, gravitational potential or a quantitative relation between clocks. They are not a formulation of relativity.

Is the Dajjal's long day physical time dilation?

The hadith describes a day like a year and instructs the community to estimate prayer times. It gives no physical cause for the length, so it cannot be treated as a relativity experiment or a way of dating creation.
