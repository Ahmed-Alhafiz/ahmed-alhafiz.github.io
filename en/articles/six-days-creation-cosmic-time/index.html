Six Days of Creation and the Age of the Universe

Creation of the heavens and earth in six days: commentators’ views of a day’s duration, the calculation in Fussilat, and what cosmic age establishes without forcing a match.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 1 September 2026English edition updated: 14 October 2026Author version 3.0Source review: 30 September 2026Independent cosmological and exegetical review: not yet completed

The answer before the details

God created the heavens and earth in six days: an explicit Quranic statement that we believe. The question needing clarification is not whether creation occurred in six days, but how long day is in these verses, and whether we can determine it using a familiar time unit. The creation verses do not specify each day’s duration, and commentators recorded more than one view. We should neither equate day with twenty-four hours without evidence nor choose an astronomical duration merely because it matches our calculations.

Cosmologists estimate the universe’s age at about 13.8 billion years within a model based on expansion, background radiation and contents. The number describes the calculated duration since the beginnings of hot expansion within that model; it does not translate day in the creation verses. Dividing 13.8 billion by six does not give us the Quranic day’s duration, just as the six-day verse supplies no equation for calculating the universe’s age.

This question comes directly from chapter one, pages 25–35, of Ahmed Alhafiz’s «قل سيروا في الأرض فانظروا كيف بدأ الخلق». The book discusses God’s power and regularities, creation’s days, verses in which a day’s duration differs, the Dajjal report, and the limits of drawing on relativity. Here we examine its argument through what exegetical texts and astronomical measurements allow, clarifying what can be affirmed and what remains open.

Why is six mentioned in the first place?

God says: “Indeed, your Lord is God, who created the heavens and earth in six days, then established Himself over the Throne … Surely creation and command belong to Him” [al-Aʿraf: 54]. In Qaf: “We created the heavens and earth and what is between them in six days, and no weariness touched Us” [Qaf: 38]. Other verses mention this in Yunus 3, Hud 7, al-Furqan 59, al-Sajdah 4 and al-Hadid 4.

Al-Aʿraf directs listeners to the Creator’s sovereignty and command, Hud connects creation with testing and then resurrection, and Qaf denies that creation’s duration arose from any weariness affecting God. Al-Tabari on Hud 7 reads creation as an argument for God’s power to bring people back after death. In saying “And no weariness touched Us,” God explicitly denies fatigue; mentioning the days must not be read by analogy with a person’s work requiring rest. Likewise, we believe what He states in “Then established Himself over the Throne,” without turning the verse into a description of a body’s motion or a physical mechanism. These verses have a purpose concerning belief in God’s power and dominion that exceeds the question of a chronological calendar.

Asking why He did not create them in an instant presumes that duration indicates inability. On pages 25–28, the book answers that presumption from within the Quran: “His command, when He wills a thing, is only to say to it: Be, and it is” [Ya-Sin: 82] affirms His will’s effectiveness, while mentioning days affirms that creation occurred according to measured ordering. The power to bring something into existence without gradual development does not require every created thing to have existed without stages. When al-Qurtubi on al-Aʿraf 54 mentions teaching gentleness and showing power step by step among the possible wisdoms, he offers an interpretive judgment, rather than a definitive text declaring this the only wisdom. The distinction remains between asking about the Creator’s power and wisdom and about the duration of a created time: answering one does not supply proof for the other.

This directly concerns the book’s thesis: for believers, causes and stages do not make regularities independent of God, and knowing causes does not cancel questions about creation and purpose. But believing this gives us no measurement of the six days’ length; a theological argument cannot yield a temporal number.

What day do we know, and what day does the verse discuss?

The modern clock divides the day into twenty-four hours; the solar day in our usage relates to the succession of night and day and Earth’s motion. In principle, a speaker can measure a duration preceding Earth using a terrestrial unit familiar today. The easy objection that Earth did not yet exist, so the verse cannot possibly mean 24 hours, is therefore unsound. But the text does not say six rotations of Earth or provide evidence requiring this measurement.

The Quran contains days of different durations according to context. God says: “A day with your Lord is like a thousand years of what you count” [al-Hajj: 47], describes the command’s ascent in a day lasting a thousand years [al-Sajdah: 5], and mentions the angels’ and Spirit’s ascent in a day lasting fifty thousand years [al-Maʿarij: 4]. These differing contexts refute the claim that every use of day necessarily means 24 hours, but do not establish that creation’s days are those mentioned in any one of them. Sharing a name does not automatically transfer duration between verses.

The exegetical tradition itself did not speak with one voice. Al-Tabari on Hud 7 records reports about the days from Sunday to Friday and transmits al-Dahhak’s statement: from the days of the hereafter, each lasting a thousand years. Al-Qurtubi on al-Aʿraf 54 records views of familiar days and days of the hereafter. Transmitting a view does not authenticate it or favor it: we cannot attribute a thousand years to all commentators or claim that they agreed on six terrestrial days.

The number is established; the duration of day here is not settled merely by transmitting these views. Acknowledging what we do not know respects the text: we neither add a number because it pleases us nor deny what the text affirms because it does not specify the duration.

The report of a day like a year: evidence of possibility, not a key to calculating creation

In Sahih Muslim’s report from al-Nawwas ibn Samʿan, the account of the Dajjal includes: “A day like a year, a day like a month, a day like a week, and the rest of his days like your days.” The Companions asked whether one day’s prayers would suffice for the day like a year. The answer was: “No, estimate its measure.” This concerns a particular time and its prayer rules; it establishes that day in a religious report does not always require the duration familiar to the questioner.

On page 30, Ahmed Alhafiz uses the report within these limits, noting on page 31 that it is not direct evidence of the duration of creation’s days. This precision is essential: the Dajjal’s year cannot be transferred to the universe’s beginning, nor can estimate its measure be made into an astronomical equation. Its value is that a day’s duration needs its context, rather than the word alone.

How does the six-day calculation work in Fussilat?

The Quran gives details in Fussilat: creating Earth in two days [41:9], mentioning mountains, blessing and the ordering of sustenance “In four days” [41:10], then completing the seven heavens in two days [41:12]. The quick calculation 2 + 4 + 2 = 8 presumes that the four form a new period added in full to the first two days. That presumption is what needs examination, rather than an explicit additional number in the verse.

Al-Tabari’s commentary on Fussilat 10 explains that the four include the two mentioned first: the creation of Earth and its contents was completed in four days overall, followed by two for the heavens, totaling six. A similar usage would be: I finished the foundation in two days, and the building was completed in four days; four is counted from the work’s beginning. Al-Qurtubi on the verse also gives the meaning completing four days and illustrates it with Arabic usage.

This is a grammatical and arithmetic answer to the objection of eight, rather than a solution to every scientific question about the sequence of celestial bodies’ formation. Earth as the planet we know did not form at the first cosmic moment, and Fussilat’s expressions have their exegetical context. Matching the first day to Earth’s formation and the final two days to particular stars’ birth requires new evidence that 2 + 2 + 2 alone does not provide.

A related question deserves a separate answer: Fussilat begins with Earth’s creation, then says “Then He turned to the heaven while it was smoke,” whereas al-Naziʿat 27–33 describes the heaven’s construction, then says “And the earth, after that, He spread it out.” Al-Tabari’s commentary on al-Naziʿat 30 distinguishes Earth’s original creation from its spreading out and bringing forth its water and pasture; Ibn Kathir also presents this reconciliation. It is an exegetical answer about the two expressions’ ordering, not an observational timeline for planet Earth’s formation. NASA states that Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago, more than nine billion years after the beginning of the estimated cosmic age. It is therefore difficult to accept a scheme placing the completed planet Earth before the observed universe’s history or before the stars preceding it. A detailed connection between Fussilat’s ordering and the astronomical sequence needs an explanation of Earth, heaven and the intended stage, with evidence for those choices. Declaring that science proved a particular ordering in the verses is insufficient.

How does science arrive at 13.8 billion years?

The age is not a number a telescope reads from a clock hanging in the sky. Researchers begin with the rate at which cosmic scales change, the model’s amounts of matter, radiation and energy, and details of the microwave background carrying traces of an early hot universe. Observations constrain the model’s parameters, then its history is calculated backward. NASA’s account of cosmic history gives an age near 13.8 billion years, while the European Space Agency’s early Planck results gave a similar estimate within the standard model. Because expansion’s pace changed over history, simply inverting today’s expansion value does not yield a precise age; the calculation needs the universe’s contents and the evolution of its expansion rate within the model.

The number has scientific strength and a limit. Its strength is that it comes from fitting measurable data to a model explaining more than one phenomenon, rather than an impression of the sky’s vastness. Its limit is that it is an estimate conditional on the model’s validity and measurement limits. Alone, it does not answer whether an earlier phase existed or establish a metaphysical judgment that all existence began from absolute nothingness. Open questions do not mean that the universe is six thousand years old or that scientists can turn verses into clocks.

Readers may think of dividing 13.8 billion years by six, yielding 2.3 billion per day. The arithmetic is correct, but has no foundation in the text or observation: the creation verses do not specify days in years, do not establish that the calculated cosmic age is identical to the sum of their durations, and the model supplies no six equal observational stages lasting 2.3 billion years each. If the estimated age changes in future, this quotient changes without changing the verse’s wording.

We must distinguish the cosmic age calculated within a scientific model, the duration of creation’s days, unspecified by the text, and a day’s duration with God in other verses. Combining all three in one multiplication misleads readers into thinking they have obtained a religious interpretation and astronomical proof simultaneously, when they have obtained neither.

Accepting the transmitted exegetical view that a creation day is a thousand years would not make it an astronomical measurement superseding or explaining 13.8 billion years. There is no evidence that the two numbers cover the same scope or that a thousand years should become a billion. If another reader calls them six epochs, which six? Cosmic history as described by models does not arrive divided into six fixed observational stages, each with boundaries matching a Quranic day. Some early-universe changes occurred in extremely short durations; stars and galaxies formed over long periods. Choosing six headings after seeing the results may serve education, but does not scientifically discover the days’ length. Our criterion is to seek evidence of the connection before putting numbers into the text.

Scientific estimation also has different levels. The microwave background’s existence and space’s expansion are observations, whereas deriving a precise age from them passes through a model and assumptions about cosmic contents. Researchers may examine assumptions or revise results, but may not erase observations because they conflict with an interpretation chosen beforehand. Conversely, they may discuss day’s meaning with commentators, but may not declare that a cosmic-age equation has settled their linguistic disagreement. Each question thus retains its testing standard rather than filling the other’s gap.

What does relativity explain about time, and what does it not determine?

Within its physical domain, relativity shows that measuring durations is affected by motion and gravity. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology, NIST explains how atomic clocks tested these results. But this does not establish that day in the creation verse is time measured near a black hole, or that the thousands in al-Hajj and al-Sajdah result from time dilation in Einstein’s equations. Such claims require specifying the observer, path and physical conditions, then evidence that the text intended that specification. Similarity between time and day does not supply it.

On page 31 of Qul Siru fi al-Ard, the book mentions relativity to encourage humility about our simple conception of time, then expressly states that it does not make the verses explanations of equations. This is a disciplined intellectual analogy, rather than evidence of the days’ length. For differences between the thousand-year and fifty-thousand-year verses and the measurement question, read Is a Day with God Like a Thousand or Fifty Thousand Years?.

The distinction is subtle but fundamental: two atomic clocks at different heights measuring slightly different durations test a particular physical prediction. That test does not answer the day described in Qaf, or how God acts or for what purpose. Belief that creation belongs to God does not depend on physicists’ inability to calculate cosmic age: a more precise calculation tomorrow would not automatically give the verse a different meaning. This protects religious inquiry from relying on a gap in scientific knowledge, and scientific inquiry from being asked for a judgment its tools do not produce.

What do we affirm, and what do we not claim with certainty?

Believers can affirm creation in six days, read in it measured ordering and power, and explore a vast universe’s history through science. They need not ignore observations or choose the days’ duration by voting among different verses. Here the strength of Qul Siru fi al-Ard is connecting time with causes and regularities without claiming that the Friedmann equation or relativity interprets the verse’s wording.

A common slogan even in defensive discourse needs correction: science proved that the six days are cosmic epochs exceeds the evidence. So does saying cosmic age definitively contradicts the Quran, because that certainty rests on the unsettled assumption that day here is twenty-four hours. Even if a commentator chooses this understanding, their argument and how they define the duration must be explained before declaring a conflict. Not every exegetical view occupies the same rank as the verse’s text.

On pages 33–34, the book brings together travel, observation, knowledge, intention and guidance. In our question this ordering has practical meaning: looking at the sky teaches what measurements say, consulting exegesis identifies what scholars did and did not say, and humility then prevents filling one field’s gap with an answer borrowed from the other. A reading begins that can receive a new discovery without redefining creation’s day every time.

Frequently asked questions

Are the six days terrestrial days lasting 24 hours each?

We believe that God created the heavens and earth in six days as He states. Specifying each as twenty-four hours, a thousand years or a particular astronomical epoch is not established merely by the word days.

Does the Quran say that one of creation’s days is a thousand years?

The six-day verses do not mention that duration. Other verses describe a day like a thousand years in other contexts, while al-Maʿarij describes a day lasting fifty thousand years. A thousand-year exegetical view was transmitted for creation’s days, but it is neither consensus nor a binding equation.

How should two days, four days and two days in Fussilat be understood?

The total is six. The four in Fussilat 10 include Earth’s two creation days in verse 9, then two days for the heavens are added. Al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi record this interpretation; it needs no invention of another two days.

Does 13.8 billion years invalidate belief in creation?

The number alone does not decide the theological issue: it estimates cosmic history within an astronomical model. It does not determine the years in each creation day, so numerical comparison alone cannot establish contradiction or correspondence.

Does the Dajjal report prove that creation’s days are long?

It does not establish their duration. It shows that a day in a religious text can have a different length in a particular context, and instructs estimating prayer times within it. Creation’s days are examined through their own verses and exegesis.
