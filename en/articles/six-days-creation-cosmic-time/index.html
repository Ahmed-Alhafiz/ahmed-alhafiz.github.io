Six Days of Creation and the Age of the Universe

The six-day count is explicit. The length of each day is not stated, and cosmology measures a different question.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 1 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent cosmological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Answer before the details

The Quran explicitly states that God created the heavens and the earth in six days. The interpretive question is what length “day” has here and whether we can assign a known unit to it. The creation passages do not state the length of each day, and classical commentators recorded more than one view. We should neither equate the term with 24 hours without argument nor assign it a cosmic length merely because a calculation appeals to us.

Cosmologists estimate the universe to be about 13.8 billion years old within a model using its expansion, the cosmic microwave background and its contents. That number describes time since the early hot expansion in that model. It is not a translation of “day” in the creation passages. Dividing 13.8 billion by six does not give the Quranic day's length, and the six days do not provide an equation for the universe's age.

The question arises from the first chapter, pages 25–35, of Ahmed Alhafiz's Qul Siru fi al-Ard. It treats divine power and regular processes, creation's days, other verses with different measures of a day, the report about the Dajjal and limits of analogy with relativity. This study tests that argument against exegesis and astronomical measurements, distinguishing settled claims from open questions.

Why mention six in the first place?

Quran 7:54 says that God created the heavens and earth in six days, then speaks of His sovereignty and command. Quran 50:38 says that God created the heavens, earth and what lies between them in six days and that no fatigue touched Him. The number also occurs in 10:3, 11:7, 25:59, 32:4 and 57:4.

Al-A'raf calls attention to the Creator's authority. Hud relates creation to a test and resurrection, and Qaf explicitly rejects the idea that making creation over days exhausted God. Al-Tabari reads the creation in 11:7 as an argument for God's power to bring people back after death. The word “days” need not be read as the work schedule of a human artisan needing rest. The reference to God's establishment over the Throne is affirmed as scripture states it; it should not be converted into a motion of a celestial body or a physical mechanism. These passages address power and rule as well as chronology.

“Why not create everything in an instant?” assumes duration indicates inability. The book responds with Quran 36:82, which describes the efficacy of God's will. The mention of days describes creation according to a measure or order; the power to create without stages does not require that every created thing appear without stages. Al-Qurtubi offers the possible wisdom of showing power successively and teaching deliberation, but that is an interpretive suggestion, not a conclusive statement of the only purpose.

Natural causes and stages do not become independent of God in the believer's view, and studying causes does not cancel the question of creation or purpose. Yet the theological point does not measure the duration of the six days. A claim about divine power is not itself a clock.

The day we know and the “day” in the verse

Modern clocks divide our day into 24 hours, and the familiar solar day is connected to Earth's rotation and the cycle of light and darkness. In principle one can express a duration before Earth existed in modern terrestrial units. Thus “Earth did not yet exist, so it is impossible for the verse to mean 24 hours” is too quick. Equally, the passage does not say “six rotations of Earth” or supply evidence requiring that measure.

The Quran uses “day” in different settings. Quran 22:47 speaks of a day with the Lord like a thousand years by human reckoning; 32:5 speaks of the command's ascent in a day of a thousand years; 70:4 describes the angels and Spirit ascending in a day measured as fifty thousand years. Their different contexts defeat the assumption that every occurrence of “day” must be 24 hours. They do not prove that creation's days are identical with any of these other days. Sharing a word does not transfer a number between passages.

Commentary is not unanimous either. On Quran 11:7, al-Tabari transmits reports of days from Sunday to Friday and a view attributed to al-Dahhak concerning days of the Hereafter, each of a thousand years. On Quran 7:54, al-Qurtubi reports both ordinary days and days of the Hereafter. Recording a view is different from endorsing it. We cannot attribute the thousand-year position to every exegete or announce a consensus on six terrestrial days.

The explicit count is six. Reports of possible lengths do not settle the measure merely by existing. Intellectual restraint means neither adding a gratifying number to scripture nor denying what it actually says because the length is unspecified.

The day like a year is an analogy, not a creation clock

In Sahih Muslim's report from al-Nawwas ibn Sam'an about the Dajjal, one day is described as like a year, another like a month and another like a week, with the rest like ordinary days. Asked whether one day's prayers would suffice in the day like a year, the Prophet instructed the community to estimate its measure. The report concerns a particular period and the practice of prayer within it. It shows that a day mentioned in a religious text need not always have the duration familiar to the questioner.

The book uses the report within that limit on page 30 and notes on page 31 that it does not directly establish the length of creation's days. We cannot transport the Dajjal's year to the beginning of the universe or make the instruction about prayer times an astronomy equation. The useful lesson is that context matters when asking how long a “day” lasts.

How do the six days add up in Quran 41?

Quran 41:9 mentions creating the earth in two days; 41:10 describes mountains, blessing and the determining of provisions “in four days”; and 41:12 mentions making the seven heavens in two days. A quick sum of 2 + 4 + 2 = 8 assumes the four are an entirely new period added to the first two. That assumption, rather than a stated additional number, is the issue.

Al-Tabari explains that the four days of 41:10 include the first two: the creation of the earth and what is in it is completed in four days altogether, followed by two for the heavens, for a total of six. Al-Qurtubi similarly treats it as the completion of four days. In ordinary speech one might say “the foundation took two days and the building was complete in four”; the latter four count from the start.

This answers the arithmetic objection. It does not settle every scientific question about the sequence of celestial formation. The planet Earth as studied by astronomy did not exist in the earliest cosmic moment. Equating a first Quranic day with the finished planet and the later two days with specific stars requires an independent argument beyond 2 + 2 + 2.

A related question concerns Quran 41's mention of earth before the heaven described as smoke, while Quran 79:27–33 speaks of the heaven and then of spreading out the earth. Al-Tabari distinguishes the original creation of earth from its subsequent spreading and the bringing forth of water and pasture; Ibn Kathir also presents this exegetical reconciliation. It addresses the texts' order but is not a direct observational timeline of planetary formation. NASA dates the Earth to roughly 4.5 billion years ago, more than nine billion years after the estimated beginning of cosmic expansion. Any detailed correspondence must say what “earth” and “heaven” mean at each stage and support that choice; one cannot merely proclaim that science has proved a preferred order in the verses.

How is the 13.8-billion-year estimate obtained?

No telescope reads that age from a clock hanging in space. Researchers measure cosmic expansion, infer the distribution of matter, radiation and dark energy within a model, and study fluctuations in the cosmic microwave background, a relic of a hot early phase. Observations constrain model parameters, and the model's history is calculated backward. NASA summarizes the universe's age as about 13.8 billion years; ESA's Planck results supplied a close estimate within the standard model.

The number is powerful because it arises from fitting measurable data to a framework that explains several phenomena. It also has a boundary: a model-dependent estimate does not by itself settle whether there was an earlier phase or the metaphysical question of absolute creation from nothing. Open questions do not make the universe six thousand years old, and the accuracy of the estimate does not turn Quranic “days” into known hours.

One might divide 13.8 billion years by six to get 2.3 billion years per day. The division is mathematically correct and interpretively unsupported. The creation verses do not equate their total duration with the age calculated by the model, nor does cosmology present six observed stages each 2.3 billion years long. If the age estimate is refined, the quotient changes while the verse's wording does not.

Even if someone adopted a transmitted interpretation that a creation day equals a thousand years, that would not become an astronomical measurement that replaces or explains the 13.8-billion-year estimate. The scopes and conversion have not been established. Calling the days six long “epochs” likewise raises a question: which six? The early universe changed rapidly, while star and galaxy formation continued across immense spans. Six descriptive headings drawn after seeing the data may help teaching, but they do not discover the length of a Quranic day.

Expansion and the microwave background are observations; extracting a precise age requires assumptions about cosmic contents and history. Researchers may scrutinize those assumptions, but cannot dismiss the observations because they dislike a chosen interpretation. Exegetes may likewise debate “day,” but cannot claim that the age equation has resolved their linguistic dispute.

What does relativity clarify, and what not?

Within its physical domain, relativity predicts that measured elapsed times differ with motion and gravity. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology has tested such differences with atomic clocks. This does not mean a creation day is a clock near a black hole or that the thousands in 22:47 and 32:5 arise from an Einstein equation. Those applications need specified observers, paths and physical circumstances, plus evidence that the verse means to describe them.

On page 31, the book invokes relativity as a reminder that a simple everyday picture of time is limited, while expressly declining to turn the verses into explanations of equations. This is an analogy inviting modesty, not a proof of the days' duration. The separate study of a thousand and fifty thousand years examines the relevant passages and measurements in detail.

Two atomic clocks at different heights can test a particular physical prediction. They do not answer how long a day in Quran 50 lasted or how and why God created. Belief in divine creation does not depend on an astronomer's uncertainty about age: a more accurate age estimate tomorrow would not automatically rewrite the verse.

What can we affirm, and what remains open?

A believer can affirm the six days of creation and their significance for power and purpose while investigating the history of a vast universe. Neither observations nor exegesis require choosing the day's length by voting among unrelated passages. At its strongest, the book connects time with causes and order without claiming that Friedmann equations or relativity define the verse.

Two slogans go beyond evidence. “Science proved the six days are cosmic epochs” has no independent match between six measured stages and the six days. “The age of the universe decisively contradicts the Quran” assumes that each day is exactly 24 hours, a claim that requires its own textual defense. One can discuss why an exegete preferred a particular meaning without giving that interpretation the status of the verse itself.

The book's invitation to travel, observe and reflect translates here into a practical discipline: the sky tells us what measurements support; exegesis tells us which readings were proposed and where they are limited; restraint stops one inquiry's unanswered questions from being filled with an answer borrowed from the other. This kind of reading can accommodate more precise discoveries without redefining a creation day each time.

Frequently asked questions

Were the six days each 24 Earth hours?

The Quran states that God created the heavens and earth in six days. Assigning each day 24 hours, a thousand years or a particular cosmic epoch does not follow simply from the word “days.”

Does the Quran say each creation day was a thousand years?

No creation passage specifies that amount. Other verses mention a thousand and fifty thousand in different settings. A thousand-year creation day is a transmitted exegetical view, not a consensus or mandatory conversion.

Why do Quran 41:9–12 mention two, four and two days?

The four in 41:10 include the first two days of 41:9, then two days are mentioned for the heavens. Al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi explain the total as six.

Does a 13.8-billion-year universe rule out creation?

The figure is an age estimate within cosmology, not a judgment on a theological question. Nor does it determine how many years are in each creation day; numerical comparison alone proves neither conflict nor correspondence.

Does the Dajjal hadith prove that creation's days were long?

It does not measure them. It shows a special context in which a day is described as long and prayer times must be estimated. Creation's days need to be studied in their own verses and commentaries.
