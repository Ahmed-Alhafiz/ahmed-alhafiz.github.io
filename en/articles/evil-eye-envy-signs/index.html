Signs of the Evil Eye: What Do Symptoms Really Prove?

The evil eye, envy and the search for an explanation: what religious texts affirm, why symptom lists need caution, and what research on comparison can actually tell us.

By Ahmed Alhafiz — أحمد الحافظEnglish edition: 28 October 2026Arabic original: 27 October 2026 · Arabic source review: 27 October 2026 · Independent specialist review: not yet completed

The story may begin with a small joy: a job after a long wait, news of a marriage, or a child whose laughter fills the phone. Then a difficult day comes, and someone says, "Didn't I tell you not to tell people?"

The old congratulations then take on a different meaning. You recall faces and words, searching the voices that wished you well for something you did not hear the first time. Fatigue becomes a question about other people's intentions, and it grows difficult to remember the blessing without remembering your fear for it.

What are the signs of the evil eye and envy? Headaches, sluggishness, insomnia or a setback are not enough, on their own, to establish that you have been affected. "The evil eye is real" is an authentic hadith, but affirming the evil eye's existence differs from diagnosing a particular person. Even a fatwa listing commonly mentioned symptoms describes them as uncertain. [1] [4]

The question "How do you know you have been affected by envy?" is understandable when fatigue continues without an explanation. But a list giving every reader a reason to be afraid may leave them further from an answer. A sign is valuable for its ability to distinguish: what does it tell us about this person, at this time, and what makes one explanation more likely than another?

What Is the Difference Between the Evil Eye and Envy?

In fatwa 21647, Islamweb defines envy as wishing for the loss of another person's blessing, and distinguishes this from being affected by the evil eye. In an answer published on Ibn Baz's website, he mentions that the evil eye may occur through admiration, without a person's choosing it. It is therefore unsound to make every expression of admiration evidence of malice, or assume that someone who praised you intended to harm you. [2]

Surah al-Falaq says: "And from the evil of an envier when he envies" [Al-Falaq 113:5]. Al-Tabari favours the general meaning of seeking refuge from the evil of every envier, without restricting it to a particular person or group. The text directs prayer towards protection from evil; it does not put in our hands a list of those who harbour it. [3]

If someone damages your reputation or obstructs your work, we have behaviour that can be described and addressed. Their intentions and any effect on your health attributed to them are separate matters. Distinguishing these preserves your ability to act: address the mistreatment where it occurs and examine illness in its own context, without making one person the explanation for everything weighing on your life.

"The Evil Eye Is Real": What Does the Hadith Establish?

Ibn 'Abbas, may God be pleased with them both, narrated that the Prophet, may God bless him and grant him peace, said: "The evil eye is real. If anything could outstrip divine decree, the evil eye would outstrip it. And when you are asked to wash, then wash." Muslim recorded it as hadith 2188. The hadith affirms the evil eye; it does not say that every headache, yawn or household dispute has this cause. [1]

Explaining "If anything could outstrip divine decree", al-Nawawi states that everything occurs by God's decree, and that harm from the evil eye or anything else occurs only in accordance with what He has decreed. The phrase therefore should not be read as making the evil eye a power that exceeds divine decree: the commentary states the opposite. [1]

The hadith has a clear function here: affirming the reality of the evil eye in religious belief and explaining that it falls within God's decree. Moving from this principle to "That person affected me when they visited" adds a name, a time and a causal relationship. These details do not occur in the hadith and require support specific to them. Belief in the principle does not require granting the details the same degree of certainty.

Are Headaches, Fatigue and Insomnia Signs of the Evil Eye and Envy?

These symptoms recur in search results, sometimes alongside frequent yawning, distress and poor appetite. Islam Question & Answer response 125543 reports signs mentioned by ruqyah practitioners, explicitly stating that they are uncertain and may be absent or vary. Honesty requires retaining this qualification when circulating the list, rather than presenting it as a decisive test for the reader. [4]

Fatigue clearly illustrates the difficulty of reasoning from a symptom alone. The UK's National Health Service lists multiple causes, including lack of sleep, stress, some medicines and health conditions. Assessment may require tests, depending on what the doctor finds. A symptom's occurrence with more than one cause is what makes its context essential. [5]

The list may grow until it includes too little sleep and too much, isolation and irritability, lethargy and a feeling of tightness in the chest. Here it is worth pausing over how it is constructed: if someone sleeping too little and someone sleeping too much can both find the same confirmation in it, how will it help them distinguish the causes of their difficulties? Broad descriptions can give a sense of recognising oneself; determining the cause remains a separate task.

An examination that does not reveal the cause leaves an open question, whose answer may require follow-up or a different assessment. The gap in knowledge should not itself become the answer. If fatigue continues for weeks or affects daily life, the NHS recommends seeing a doctor. [5]

When a Label Comes Before Listening to the Person

The search for an explanation takes a sharply human form in a scene from Umm Abbas: Bringing the Beloved and Reuniting a Divorced Woman with Her Husband. In chapter eight, a man speaks in the language of envy and magical binding, claiming that girls are more vulnerable to both. The mother receives his words as though hearing a treatment; the father listens as though counting mistakes. These are more than obscure words: this is a family wanting to know how to help their daughter. [8]

When Maryam mocks him, the man says that what is inside her likes to laugh. The father replies, "She is mocking because she is afraid." Both statements address the same behaviour but give it different meanings: the father sees a response to her fear; the other man attributes it to what he claims is inside her.

In my reading of the scene, its cruelty lies in the moment Maryam loses the authority to speak about herself. If she laughs, her laughter becomes evidence; if she is afraid, her fear is reinterpreted from within the same claim. The question then becomes: what description can she offer that will be heard as her own?

The scene is fictional. Its connection to our subject is this problem of listening and inference, rather than proving or disproving the evil eye. When an explanation accepts every response as confirmation, someone helping a patient needs to pause: what have we actually learned from their experience, and what have we put into it ourselves?

Are Marriage Delays and Household Problems Signs of Envy?

This explanation may spread from an individual's body to the entire home: an engagement is delayed, a couple quarrel and the family's income falls, all brought together under "the evil eye has struck the household". We then lose the distinctions that might lead to a solution for each problem.

For marriage, examine the stated reasons for rejection, expectations and compatibility; for conflict, look at what recurs between the two people; for financial difficulty, examine income and commitments. This reviews what can be acted upon while leaving room for prayer and seeking well-being.

If there is harmful interference, identify the act, its effect and the boundaries you set in response. This keeps the problem open to discussion, without making someone you disagreed with responsible for everything you have missed.

What Do Studies Say About Envy and Comparison?

When psychology studies envy, it begins with how people describe their experience and what can be measured of their response. This matters before borrowing its findings to explain what others harbour towards us.

In a 2009 paper, Niels van de Ven, Marcel Zeelenberg and Rik Pieters used three studies to distinguish an experience directing desire towards improving one's own position from another directing it towards lowering the position of the better-off person. In the Spanish study, researchers followed forty-nine participants for two weeks. Thirty-nine who reported experiencing envy were included in the analysis and were asked to describe it on the evening it occurred. This brought the report closer to the experience rather than relying on distant recall. [6]

The data remained people's own reports of their feelings and motivations, across three Western settings. The findings provide a basis for distinguishing two experiences. Judging someone's intention from their silence or their glance, however, was not a test the researchers conducted. Nor do their English labels become Islamic legal categories simply by being translated into Arabic.

A 2023 review by Fabio Carraturo and colleagues included nine articles on comparison, envy and depression on social networking sites. Seven studies were cross-sectional, measuring variables at one point in time and making it difficult to establish which came first. With this design, a correlation between the two feelings does not draw a causal arrow from one to the other. [7]

The review interpreted the two studies whose designs allowed direction to be examined as showing that depression predicts more comparison and envy, rather than the reverse. Given the small number of studies and their heavy reliance on self-reports, this direction should be generalised cautiously. Nor does the finding mean that everyone experiencing envy is depressed, or that every comparison harms the person making it.

The research's usefulness and limits thus become clear together: it brings us closer to understanding the experience of comparison and how it varies by person and context. But it does not test the supernatural evil eye or turn the fatigue you feel into evidence of another person's emotions.

How Can You Handle Fear of the Evil Eye Without Accusing Others?

Record what happens, when, and what affects it, rather than starting with the names of those who congratulated you. If the symptom concerns your health, describe it as well as you can and seek an appropriate assessment. If the problem concerns a relationship, examine what is happening within it. To explore fear's effect on bodily experience, see "Can Fear Cause Real Physical Symptoms?"

God says: "Avoid much suspicion; indeed, some suspicion is sinful" [Al-Hujurat 49:12]. Al-Tabari's commentary prohibits thinking ill of believers and seeking out their private affairs. Investigating a cause does not require turning a relative's silence or lukewarm congratulations into testimony about their heart. [9]

You may choose to share less of your life for the sake of privacy. But that choice does not establish that everyone who knows of your blessing poses a danger to you. You can preserve some space for yourself while also leaving room for joy that does not require an apology every time.

Permissible Ruqyah for the Evil Eye: Seeking Well-Being

Sahih Muslim records, in Abu Sa'id's narration, Gabriel's prayer for the Prophet, may God bless him and grant him peace: "In God's name I recite ruqyah for you, against everything that harms you, against the evil of every soul or envious eye. May God heal you. In God's name I recite ruqyah for you." In 'Awf ibn Malik's hadith: "There is no harm in ruqyah so long as it involves no associating partners with God." [10] [11]

The prayer conveys the breadth of seeking well-being: "against everything that harms you". A person can pray, seek permissible ruqyah and continue a health assessment without making a name for the cause a condition for beginning. Improvement itself calls for gratitude; it does not identify the person to whom the harm has been attributed.

Some of what happened may remain without a full explanation. You can say, "I am tired and need help", and say, "I do not yet know why." Between these sentences there is room for faith, treatment and preserving affection. Do not let fear for a blessing take away everything that made it a blessing.
