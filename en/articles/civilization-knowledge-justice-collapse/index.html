Why Civilizations Collapse

Water and knowledge enable settlement; institutions and justice shape resilience when drought, war or other shocks arrive.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 26 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent cosmological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Short answer

Civilizations rarely collapse for one reason. Drought or flood may put pressure on food supplies, war may cut trade, and salinity may weaken agriculture; but the effect of a shock depends on a society's institutions. Do they measure danger early? Do they distribute water and food fairly? Do they repair canals? Do people trust the records and the authorities? Can knowledge be passed on after those who possess it have died?

The Quran does not offer a material equation to replace history. It adds an ethical measure: a blessing is no certificate of survival, power is no proof of divine approval, and civilization may express gratitude or become an instrument of tyranny. This is the thesis of the third chapter of Qul Siru fi al-Ard: water creates the possibility of civilization, institutions turn that possibility into settlement, and justice determines whom that settlement serves. Collapse is often the outcome of interactions among environment, power and institutional failure.

Before asking why it falls: what is a civilization?

It is more than grand buildings or advanced tools. People may carve rock and still act unjustly, master accounting yet use a register for predatory taxation, or build a great dam and then fail to maintain or share its water.

Civilization is an interconnected network:

Water, food and energy; knowledge and writing; markets and law; trust and authority; and values that determine the purpose and limits of power.

The third chapter, pp. 55–59, therefore begins by rejecting a sightseeing approach to ancient remains. An artifact says more than 'they were powerful': it opens harder questions. Who worked? Who owned the surplus? Who recorded shares? How were disputes settled? Why did the system survive or disappear?

This definition prevents two errors: reducing civilization to its material objects, and explaining its collapse through a moral sermon detached from water, food and administration.

From water to a state: the chain stone does not show

Water is necessary for life, but a river does not become a civilization merely by existing. Between river and city lies a chain of labor and knowledge:

  1. Water that can be anticipated, cultivation and a storable surplus.
  2. A surplus that supports specialization and division of labor.
  3. Specialization needs measurement, writing, administration and law.
  4. Administration that creates power and puts it to the test of justice.

When a society can hold back or direct water and defer part of a harvest until the next season, surplus appears. Surplus is more than 'extra food': it is social time that allows builders, scribes, soldiers, doctors and craftspeople to exist without each growing their own food.

A storehouse that protects people from drought can also subordinate them. A register that preserves ownership of a field can falsify it. A canal that requires cooperation may be managed by agreement or coercion. Irrigation therefore does not automatically lead to a centralized despotic state. Historical studies have criticized the 'hydraulic hypothesis' that makes irrigation a direct cause of despotism, showing, for example, that the image of central control in Egyptian discourse does not mean everyday water management was entirely centralized.[1]

The more precise formulation is not 'the river created the state': water management created problems of coordination, maintenance and distribution, and institutions were one of the human responses to them.

Why do measurement and writing become part of bread?

As society expands, oral memory alone is insufficient. Fields, debts, shares and work schedules must be recorded, measures standardized, and one season compared with another. The book therefore connects surplus, measurement and the pen on pp. 66–77.

This does not mean writing arose from one economic cause, or that every ancient record was a tax record. It means complex civilization needs memory beyond the individual. Here the third article connects with the second: the pen that preserved knowledge became a tool of administration, and then a matter of trustworthiness.

A number is not fair in itself. Standardized measures can protect the market, while precise accounts can also perfect exploitation. The Quranic demand for honest weights and measures belongs within economic life rather than at its decorative margin.

Justice is infrastructure, not decoration

Justice may appear to be a value that comes after roads and storehouses are built, but the third chapter reverses that order: justice is part of the infrastructure that makes them work.

When records are corrupted, trust weakens. When trust weakens, every transaction becomes more costly: merchants need more guards, creditors stronger guarantees, rulers greater coercion, and farmers reasons to conceal their output. Walls may still stand at this stage, but the capacity to cooperate has begun to erode.

This does not mean there is a simple historical law under which every unjust state falls immediately or every just state avoids disaster. Injustice may endure for a long time, and the just may be put to the test. The point is narrower and stronger: when institutions become a means of plundering what they are supposed to protect, they squander the trust, information and capacity for collective action on which civilization rests.

The Quran weighs a community by security, provision and conduct, as in God's words:

'And God sets forth the example of a town that was secure and at ease, its provision coming abundantly from every place. It was ungrateful for God's blessings, so God made it taste the garment of hunger and fear because of what its people used to do.' [Al-Nahl: 112].

Commentators mention Mecca when applying the parable, while the verse's structure remains striking: security and provision, then hunger and fear, with the consequence attributed to people's actions.[2] But it is not a tool for attaching an unseen judgment to every contemporary famine or war. Judging a particular event requires a scriptural text or evidence, not merely a rhetorical resemblance.

Ancient Egypt: the Nile as resource and power, not a complete explanation

In ancient Egypt the Nile was no geographical backdrop. The relative regularity of its agricultural floods, measurement of land and crops, and organization of labor, storage and taxation all contributed to a highly complex system.

But 'the Nile made Egypt' compresses human beings and institutions into a watercourse. The Nile offered opportunities and imposed dangers; it did not determine the form of the state on its own. Research on Ptolemaic Egypt indicates that reduced Nile flooding associated with volcanic eruptions coincided with economic and political disruption and revolts. It does not say climate was the only actor: the shock passed through taxation, war and relationships between elites and society.[3]

An important principle appears here: natural danger tests institutions rather than replacing them. A low flood may remain a limited crisis if stores, information and capable distribution exist; it may become widespread unrest when it coincides with war, harsh taxation and lost trust.

The book reads Pharaoh's story from this angle. His words, 'Does not the kingdom of Egypt belong to me, and these rivers flowing beneath me?' [Al-Zukhruf: 51], are no lesson in irrigation history: they are a discourse of power that incorporates the resource into the image of kingship. The Quranic criticism does not condemn water or organization. It exposes the moment when putting resources to use becomes a claim of absolute sovereignty.

Mesopotamia: when irrigation is both solution and problem

The Tigris and Euphrates enabled agriculture, transport, cities, writing and administration. Yet irrigation in an arid environment brings the risk of salt accumulation if groundwater rises and drainage is poor. Salinity and soil degradation are therefore among the pressures on agriculture in parts of Mesopotamia.[4]

“Salt destroyed Sumer” overstates the evidence. Its history includes shifting rivers, conflicts among cities, trade changes, invasions, changing centers of power and human adaptation or movement. Salt was one factor within a system, not a master key explaining centuries.

The example shows a tool's unintended effect. Irrigation that increased yields can weaken soil if maintenance, drainage and accumulated knowledge fail. Nature is not a moral enemy and technique is not a guarantee. Outcomes depend on management across time.

Saba and Marib: where does evidence end and interpretation begin?

God says:

'For Saba there was a sign in their dwelling place: two gardens, to the right and left. Eat from your Lord's provision and give thanks to Him: a good land and a forgiving Lord. But they turned away, so We sent upon them the flood of al-Arim…' [Saba: 15–16].

The remains at Marib establish a water-management and agricultural system of great importance. UNESCO describes the Marib dam and the irrigation system that created a large agricultural oasis and required engineering knowledge and a complex organizational framework.[5]

This meets a Quranic picture that combines provision, water and gratitude before the scene changes. But material remains do not establish every detail of exegetical accounts, or allow certainty that a single archaeological collapse at a particular date was the only 'flood of al-Arim.' Some old reports about the cause of a breach are transmitted in commentaries; this does not turn them into engineering reports that archaeology can establish.[6]

The stronger statement is that a clear geographical and civilizational relationship connects Saba, Marib and water-based settlement, while the mechanism and detailed date of the Quranic event remain beyond what the available material evidence settles.

Thamud and al-Hijr: a warning about names and archaeological layers

The Quran mentions Thamud and their carving homes in mountains, making their built strength a matter for reflection rather than commendation. Yet the site now known as al-Hijr or Madain Salih has a multilayered history. According to UNESCO, its most prominent preserved remains, especially the carved tombs, are Nabataean and date mainly to the first century BCE and first century CE.[7]

It is therefore wrong to present every famous rock facade at al-Hijr as a 'house of Thamud' established by direct archaeological evidence. The geographical memory, Quranic name and traditions associated with the place can be described while distinguishing them from the dating of buildings examined by archaeologists.

This is no marginal point. It applies the book's own method: looking does not mean making a remnant say what we want, but respecting its layers and the limits of our knowledge.

Does injustice bring down civilizations scientifically?

Historical research can examine observable changes: disrupted production, inefficient administration, revolts, unequal exposure to risk, broken trade networks, migration and abandoned cities. It cannot measure “sin” with an instrument or independently prove a particular disaster is divine punishment.

The Quran can affirm that particular peoples were punished for their rejection and injustice, and set out general ethical principles concerning gratitude, justice and corruption. But it does not exempt us from examining soil, inscriptions, climate and economics, or permit us to condemn the victims of every catastrophe.

Comparative studies of historical collapse stress that pressures have different outcomes. Some societies underwent profound change; others absorbed shocks through environmental, political or cultural resilience. Their paths were complex and unpredictable.[8]

There are therefore two levels that should neither be merged nor separated entirely:

Question What historical research can say What the Quranic measure adds
Why did food decline? Rain, floods, soil, war, trade, administration A blessing is a trust; gratitude is action, not words alone
Why did the response fail? Weak information, maintenance, coordination, legitimacy and distribution Injustice, corruption and arrogance are not peripheral matters
Is the disaster punishment? The unseen cannot be established from material remains alone Affirm it where scripture reports it, not by guessing in every event
Does collapse mean the end of a people? A state may fall while communities and cultures remain and transform Learn from the outcome rather than glorifying transient power

Where do text and science meet, and where not?

They meet in rejecting a fantasy of guaranteed strength. A dam needs repair, a river needs measurement, soil needs management and a state needs trust. The Quran adds that possession of those tools does not place their owner beyond accountability.

The questions and evidence still differ. Archaeology reconstructs possibilities from layers, inscriptions, grains and climate measurements; revelation offers moral and purposeful meaning and relates particular unseen matters no laboratory can extract. A forced conflict begins when a verse is treated as a complete excavation report, or an excavation is asked to rule out every meaning beyond matter.

The careful formulation is:

A material cause explains the path by which an event occurred without cancelling the ethical question. Religious meaning does not permit inventing a material cause that evidence has not established.

A model of interacting failure

Rather than search for one culprit, the third chapter offers a feedback sequence:

  1. Environmental or external pressure: drought, flood, epidemic, war or a change in trade.
  2. Production loss: poorer harvests, higher prices and less surplus.
  3. Institutional choice: repair, distribution and adaptation, or denial, plunder and delay.
  4. Trust crisis: evasion, disputes, revolt, migration and less reliable information.
  5. Decaying maintenance and knowledge: canals go unrepaired, records become unreliable and skills fail to transfer.
  6. A final shock: it looks like the sole cause although it struck an already fragile system.

The model does not fit every society in one way. It guards against three simplifications: climate does everything, morality acts without material channels, or technology saves society regardless of how it is used.

The book's distinct contribution

The third chapter's strength lies not in recounting Egypt, Mesopotamia, Saba and Thamud one after another, but in finding a single question within them:

How does a natural blessing become an institution, and how does that institution become a test of justice?

Pages 60–77 build the chain from water and farming to surplus, measurement and writing. Pages 78–83 show that technology cannot survive without institutions that transmit it. Pages 84–95 then test the idea in Egypt, Mesopotamia, Saba, Thamud and 'Ad, before pages 96–104 bring collapse together in a compound picture: environment, power, memory and justice.

This clarifies the article's place within the whole book. The first chapter presented a universe in which regular processes operate; the second introduced human beings capable of learning them; the third shows how that knowledge becomes settlement and power. The fourth will enter matter itself—the cell, iron, fire and light—to ask: does knowledge deepen a person's reverent fear of God, or enlarge their capacity for corruption?

Final assessment

Why do civilizations collapse?

Because they are interconnected systems, not solid stones. A crisis may begin with rain, soil or war, but it grows when institutions fail to perceive, maintain and distribute, and when power turns from protecting cooperation to preying on society. A civilization under pressure may survive if it preserves knowledge, trust and the capacity to adapt.

The Quranic view does not reduce this to geography. It asks who possessed water, surplus, records and decisions: did they give thanks for the blessing by upholding its rightful obligations, or turn it into a justification for arrogance? The book therefore does not say civilization falls because it did not know. It warns against knowledge without trustworthiness, administration without justice, and power without balance.

The question left by ruins is more than “how did they build this?” It is also “for whom was it built, by what measure was it used, and what survived when that measure was lost?”

Frequently asked questions

Is drought the main cause of civilizational collapse?

It can be a severe pressure, but it produces no single outcome. Stores, trade, allocation, legitimacy and adaptability change the result. Serious inquiry avoids environmental determinism.

Does injustice always bring down a state quickly?

No. Injustice may endure for a long time, but its persistence increases the cost of coercion and weakens trust, information and cooperation. Religious texts establish its responsibility and consequence; the timing of a particular state's fall cannot be extracted from a general slogan.

Is the Marib dam the one in the story of Saba?

Saba's archaeological settlement at Marib is associated with a great dam-and-irrigation system, matching the verses' general civilizational setting. But equating a particular archaeological collapse at a particular date with the flood of al-Arim with certainty goes beyond what the material evidence settles.

Are Madain Salih the houses of Thamud?

The place is historically and geographically associated with the name al-Hijr, but its most famous surviving facades are dated to the Nabataean period. Not every visible remnant can be attributed to Thamud without distinguishing the site's layers.

Can archaeology prove divine punishment?

It may document flood, fire, abandonment or economic deterioration, but cannot measure divine intent. Punishment is affirmed where revelation states it, not assigned to every disaster by conjecture.
