Why Civilizations Collapse

Water and knowledge enable settlement; institutions and justice shape resilience when drought, war or other shocks arrive.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 26 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent cosmological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Short answer

Civilizations rarely collapse for one reason. Drought or flood can threaten food, war can sever trade, and salt can weaken fields. Yet the effect of a shock depends on institutions: do they identify danger early, distribute water and food fairly, maintain canals, keep trustworthy records and pass knowledge to later generations?

The Quran does not offer a material equation to replace history. It adds an ethical measure: abundance does not guarantee survival; power does not prove approval; building can embody gratitude or become an instrument of oppression. This is the third chapter's argument in Qul Siru fi al-Ard. Water makes civilization possible; institutions turn possibility into settlement and cooperation; justice determines whom it serves. Collapse often emerges from interactions between environment, power and institutional failure.

Before asking why it falls: what is a civilization?

It is more than grand buildings or advanced tools. People may carve rock and still act unjustly, master accounting yet use a register for predatory taxation, or build a great dam and then fail to maintain or share its water.

A civilization is a connected system of water and food, skills and division of work, measurement and records, institutions and law, trust and the capacity to respond. These parts interact. A monument is a visible remnant, while relations among workers, stores, rulers and households are harder to reconstruct.

The book's third chapter, pages 55–59, refuses a sightseeing approach to remains. An artifact says more than “they were strong.” It prompts questions: who worked, who owned the surplus, who recorded shares, how disputes were settled, and why a system endured or ceased. This avoids reducing a civilization to its material objects or explaining its end with a moral slogan detached from food, water and administration.

From water to a state: the chain stone does not show

Water is necessary for life, but a river does not become a civilization merely by existing. Between river and city lies a chain of labor and knowledge:

  1. Water that can be anticipated, cultivation and a storable surplus.
  2. A surplus that supports specialization and division of labor.
  3. Specialization that needs measurement, writing, administration and rules.
  4. Administration that creates power and puts it to the test of justice.

When a society can direct water and set aside some yield for another season, it has more than “extra food.” It has social time for builders, scribes, soldiers, doctors and makers who do not each grow all their own meals.

The store that shelters people from hunger can also subordinate them. A register that protects ownership can falsify it; a canal needing cooperation can be managed by consent or coercion. Irrigation does not automatically create a central tyranny. Historical studies criticize the simple “hydraulic hypothesis” of direct causation and show, for example, that Egypt's image of central control does not mean all everyday water management was centrally directed. The careful claim is that managing water created problems of coordination, repair and allocation, to which institutions were among human responses.

Why do measurement and writing become part of bread?

As a society grows, oral memory alone cannot handle every field, debt, allocation or work schedule. Units need agreement, accounts need comparison and one season needs to be set beside another. Pages 66–77 of the book connect surplus, measurement and the pen.

This does not claim writing was born from one economic cause or that every ancient record was a tax document. It means complex cooperation benefits from memory beyond any one person. The study of writing shows how the pen that preserves knowledge also becomes an administrative tool and an ethical responsibility.

A number is not just by itself. Common measures can protect exchange, while precise accounts can also perfect exploitation. The Quranic demand for honest weights and measures belongs within economic life rather than at its decorative margin.

Justice is infrastructure, not decoration

Justice may look like a value added after roads and granaries are built. The third chapter reverses this: fair dealing is part of the structure that lets them function. Corrupt registers weaken trust. When trust weakens, each transaction costs more: merchants need stronger protection, lenders demand tougher guarantees, rulers rely on more coercion, and farmers hide yield. Walls may remain standing while cooperation erodes.

This is not a simple law that every unjust state falls at once or every just state escapes disaster. Injustice can endure for a long time; a fair community can still face catastrophe. The narrower and stronger argument is that institutions that prey on those they should protect squander trustworthy information, mutual confidence and collective capacity.

Quran 16:112 offers a parable of a secure community receiving provision, then experiencing hunger and fear. Commentators discuss Mecca in its application. The sequence of security, provision, hunger and fear is striking, but it is not a tool to label every modern famine or war a divine punishment. A judgment about a particular event requires appropriate evidence, not a resemblance in rhetoric.

Ancient Egypt: the Nile as resource and power, not a complete explanation

The Nile was more than a scenic background. Flood patterns, surveying fields and crops, organizing labor and stores, and collecting dues all entered a complex social system. “The Nile made Egypt” compresses human action and institutions into one river. It offered opportunities and imposed hazards without writing the state's form on its own.

Research on Ptolemaic Egypt links lower Nile floods associated with volcanic eruptions to economic and political unrest and revolts. It does not make climate the only actor: the shock worked through taxation, war and relations among elites and society. A low flood can remain a contained crisis where stores, data and distribution function; it can amplify unrest where war, harsh levies and distrust already exist.

The book reads Pharaoh's boast in Quran 43:51, that Egypt's kingdom and rivers belong to him, as a claim of political power over a resource. The Quran does not condemn water or its organization. It exposes a moment when control is turned into a claim of absolute sovereignty.

Mesopotamia: when irrigation is both solution and problem

The Tigris and Euphrates supported farming, transport, cities, writing and administration. In an arid environment, however, irrigation can raise groundwater and leave salts in the soil where drainage is poor. Salinity and land degradation are among pressures examined for parts of Mesopotamia.

“Salt destroyed Sumer” overstates the evidence. Its history includes shifting rivers, conflicts among cities, trade changes, invasions, changing centers of power and human adaptation or movement. Salt was one factor within a system, not a master key explaining centuries.

The example shows a tool's unintended effect. Irrigation that increased yields can weaken soil if maintenance, drainage and accumulated knowledge fail. Nature is not a moral enemy and technique is not a guarantee. Outcomes depend on management across time.

Saba and Marib: where does evidence end and interpretation begin?

Quran 34:15–16 describes Saba's gardens, provision, turning away and the flood of al-Arim. Remains at Marib attest a significant irrigation and cultivation system. UNESCO describes its dam and canals creating an extensive oasis requiring engineering skill and complex organization.

This setting resonates with the Quranic picture of prosperity, water, gratitude and change. Archaeology does not verify every detail of later commentary or identify one dated breach as the only “flood of al-Arim.” Reports about a small animal undermining a barrier occur in commentary, but transmission of a story does not make it an archaeologically established engineering cause.

The sound claim is a strong geographic and civilizational association between Saba, Marib and its waterworks. The precise mechanism and date of the Quranic event remain beyond what the surviving material can settle. The dedicated Marib study examines the inscriptions and repairs in detail.

Thamud and al-Hijr: a warning about names and archaeological layers

The Quran speaks of Thamud carving mountain houses, making their built strength a subject of reflection rather than a certificate of virtue. The site now called al-Hijr or Madain Salih has a layered history. According to UNESCO, its best-preserved monumental tombs are Nabataean, mainly dating to the first century BCE and first century CE.

One should therefore not present each celebrated rock facade as “a house of Thamud” on direct archaeological evidence. The area's religious memory can be explained while separating it from the date and makers of individual structures. This is an application of the book's method: looking at remains means respecting what their layers can and cannot tell us. The Hegra study develops the distinction.

Does injustice bring down civilizations scientifically?

Historical research can examine observable changes: disrupted production, inefficient administration, revolts, unequal exposure to risk, broken trade networks, migration and abandoned cities. It cannot measure “sin” with an instrument or independently prove a particular disaster is divine punishment.

The Quran, for its part, states that particular peoples faced consequences for denial and wrongdoing and sets ethical themes of gratitude, fairness and corruption. That does not excuse ignoring soils, inscriptions, climate and economics, or blaming the victims of every catastrophe.

Comparative work on historical collapse stresses that similar pressures do not have identical results. Some societies changed profoundly; others absorbed shocks through ecological, political or cultural resilience. Paths can be complex and unpredictable. Thus the historical and ethical levels should be read without collapsing one into the other.

Where do text and science meet, and where not?

They meet in rejecting a fantasy of guaranteed strength. A dam needs repair, a river needs measurement, soil needs management and a state needs trust. The Quran adds that possession of those tools does not place their owner beyond accountability.

The questions and evidence still differ. Archaeology reconstructs possibilities from layers, inscriptions, seeds and climate proxies; revelation offers moral and purposeful meaning and relates particular unseen matters no laboratory can extract. A forced conflict begins when a verse is treated as a complete excavation report, or an excavation is asked to rule out every meaning beyond matter.

The disciplined approach is to establish what each source actually says, distinguish a physical mechanism from a moral assessment, and make any link between the two with an explicit warrant and a stated level of confidence.

A model of interacting failure

Rather than search for one culprit, the third chapter offers a feedback sequence:

  1. External or environmental pressure: drought, flood, disease, war or trade changes.
  2. Production loss: poorer harvests, higher prices and less surplus.
  3. Institutional choice: repair, distribution and adaptation, or denial, extraction and delay.
  4. Trust crisis: evasion, disputes, revolt, migration and less reliable information.
  5. Decaying maintenance and knowledge: canals go unrepaired, records become unreliable and skills fail to transfer.
  6. A final shock: it looks like the sole cause although it struck an already fragile system.

The model does not fit every society in one way. It guards against three simplifications: climate does everything, morality acts without material channels, or technology saves society regardless of how it is used.

The book's distinct contribution

The third chapter is stronger than a list of Egypt, Mesopotamia, Saba and Thamud. It asks one recurring question across them: how do water, knowledge, institutions and justice interact? Pages 60–77 move from water and farming to surplus, measurement and writing; pages 78–83 show that technique requires an institution capable of transmitting it. Pages 84–95 test the view across several places; pages 96–104 assemble environment, power, memory and justice in a compound account of collapse.

This gives the article its place in the whole book. The first chapter describes a universe of regular processes. The second considers humans able to learn them. The third shows how learning becomes settlement and power. The fourth turns to matter itself, from the cell to iron, fire and light, to ask whether greater knowledge deepens responsibility or enlarges the capacity for harm.

Final assessment

Why do civilizations fall? Because they are interdependent systems, not solid blocks of stone. A crisis may begin with rain, soil or war, then deepen when institutions fail to perceive, maintain and distribute, or when authority turns from protecting cooperation to preying on society. A pressured community can also endure by preserving knowledge, trust and the ability to adapt.

The Quranic view does not reduce this to geography. It asks who controlled water, surplus, records and decisions: were those blessings used to establish rights or to justify pride? The book does not say civilizations fall merely because they lacked knowledge. It warns against knowledge without trustworthiness, administration without justice and power without measure.

The question left by ruins is more than “how did they build this?” It is also “for whom was it built, by what measure was it used, and what survived when that measure was lost?”

Frequently asked questions

Is drought the main cause of civilizational collapse?

It can be a severe pressure, but it produces no single outcome. Stores, trade, allocation, legitimacy and adaptability change the result. Serious inquiry avoids environmental determinism.

Does injustice always bring down a state quickly?

No. It can last a long time while increasing coercion's cost and weakening information, trust and cooperation. A general moral claim cannot date the fall of a particular state.

Is the Marib dam the one in the story of Saba?

Marib's historical irrigation system strongly fits the setting of Saba's cultivated landscape. Equating one precisely dated archaeological breach with the Quranic flood of al-Arim as a certainty goes beyond the surviving evidence.

Are Madain Salih's famous facades houses of Thamud?

Al-Hijr is associated with Thamud in Islamic sources, but the most prominent surviving facades are dated to the Nabataean phase. Each archaeological layer must be distinguished.

Can archaeology prove divine punishment?

It may document flood, fire, abandonment or economic deterioration, but cannot measure divine intent. Punishment is affirmed where revelation states it, not assigned to every disaster by conjecture.

Limits of this edition

This is an independent research article led by Ahmed Alhafiz, not a peer-reviewed academic paper. The sources and argument were reviewed internally. Independent archaeological, historical and exegetical review has not yet been completed.

Evidence transparency

Environmental and archaeological sources describe measurable pressures and structures. Quranic passages and commentary address meaning and ethical responsibility. No generic model assigns divine judgment to a contemporary disaster or predicts a state collapse date.
