Does Quran 51:47 Describe an Expanding Universe?

Astronomy measures expanding distances. The verse speaks of building the heaven and breadth, with several exegetical readings.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 28 September 2026English edition updated: 15 October 2026Independent cosmological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Direct answer

The universe expands on large scales; identifying that expansion with Quran 51:47 is an interpretive claim that needs separate evidence. Galaxy spectra and distances, the brightness history of supernovae and the cosmic microwave background support a coherent model. Quran 51:47 says, in a close English rendering, “We built the heaven with strength, and indeed We are lamusi'un.” It does not name galaxies or their recession rate. Al-Tabari interpreted lamusi'un in terms of breadth of power and also transmitted Ibn Zayd's “He widened it”; other exegetes offered related senses. A contemporary reader may reflect on a possible resonance with cosmic expansion. Claiming that the verse unambiguously states continuous expansion or predicts Hubble's law requires stronger evidence than a shared word for breadth.

Where this appears in Qul Siru fi al-Ard
This study extends pages 21–25 of the book, which discuss expansion, the scale factor, redshift and the cosmic background, then set limits for comparisons between revelation and a scientific model. Those pages do not interpret Quran 51:47 directly. We examine it because readers of the cosmic chapter naturally ask about this verse. The manuscript starts the inquiry; commentary and observation test its general claims.

What does an expanding universe mean?

It does not mean galaxies were ejected from a central point into empty space surrounding the cosmos, nor that everything grows in the same way. General relativity describes large-scale cosmic geometry changing with time. Cosmologists use the scale factor to describe changes in distances between positions that follow the overall expansion. When it increases, two distant galaxies without a strong local gravitational bond become farther apart.

Dots on a stretching surface offer an imperfect analogy: distances between the dots increase without a center on that surface. The universe is not literally the surface of a balloon in a room, but the analogy explains why distant observers can each see others receding on average without occupying the center.

The solar system, a person and an atom do not expand with the cosmic flow. Electromagnetic bonds and local gravity hold such structures together. Expansion becomes most apparent between distant groups not bound as one local system.

On page 22 the book introduces the scale factor and a Friedmann equation. For a general reader, their role is to describe how distances change over time in a model with approximately homogeneous large-scale contents. Writing an equation does not by itself prove that a model fits our universe. Its strength is explaining several observations together and remaining open to comparison with new data. “Space expands” is a mathematical description on immense scales, not a rubbery substance pushing every nearby object. NASA explains that cosmological redshift stretches the wavelengths of light during its long journey.

How do we observe expansion without seeing space move?

Light carries a pattern of wavelengths. Astronomers compare familiar spectral lines in a distant galaxy's light with lines measured in laboratories. A shift toward longer wavelengths is called redshift; on cosmological scales the expansion during the light's journey stretches those wavelengths.

The scale factor discussed in the book becomes understandable through light itself. In a homogeneous cosmological model, after isolating local motion and gravitational effects, the ratio of observed to emitted wavelength equals the ratio of the scale factor at observation to that at emission: 1 + z = a(today) / a(emission), where z is redshift. If the scale factor doubles during the journey, the wavelength doubles. This does not directly measure the whole universe’s size, nor turn every large redshift into an ordinary speed through fixed space. The relationship is an interpretation within a cosmological model with its conditions.

Edwin Hubble's 1929 work assembled published radial-velocity measurements and galaxy-distance estimates to show an approximate relation: larger distance generally accompanied greater measured recession. The nearby-universe relation is often written as recession rate approximately equal to the Hubble parameter times distance. The early data were imprecise by today's standards, and the theory and observations had many contributors; the paper nevertheless became a landmark for a dynamic universe.

Redshift needs a distance ladder. Astronomers use calibrated variable stars, Type Ia supernovae, geometric methods and other indicators. Their confidence comes from converging observations, not from a single image or telescope.

Distinguish measurement from its interpretation. An astronomer compares a known spectral line’s position in galaxy light with its laboratory wavelength: this change is measurable. Estimating distance and using a model are necessary to read distant galaxies’ redshifts as expansion history. The European Space Agency explains that cosmological redshift is not simply the familiar Doppler effect of a source moving within fixed space; local velocities can also affect nearby objects’ spectra. One redshifted line is therefore not self-sufficient proof of the universe’s entire age or nature.

What evidence makes expansion a robust model?

Evidence Measurement What it supports
Redshift and distance Spectral wavelengths compared with distance indicators Recession related to distance in the nearby universe
Type Ia supernovae Calibrated brightness across cosmic epochs Expansion history and late-time acceleration
Cosmic microwave background Early radiation, temperature and fine fluctuations A hot, dense early phase and model parameters
Baryon acoustic oscillations A statistical ruler in galaxy distribution Changes in distances and expansion over time
Light-element abundances Hydrogen, helium and deuterium ratios A hot early phase consistent with the expansion history

ESA’s Planck satellite mapped the cosmic microwave background with high precision. It does not show an “explosion” itself; the radiation was released when the early universe became transparent, hundreds of thousands of years after its hot beginning. Its fluctuation pattern helps test cosmic geometry, contents and expansion history.

Thus expansion does not hang on a single interpretation of one redshift. A model joins galaxy spectra, supernova brightness, large-scale clustering, early radiation and elemental abundances. Any revision must still explain the full pattern.

Is expansion constant or accelerating?

The rate has not remained constant across cosmic history. Matter, radiation and other components contribute differently over time. Measurements of distant supernovae in the late 1990s showed that expansion in the more recent universe accelerates rather than merely slowing under the gravity of matter. “Dark energy” is a working name for the phenomenon or component invoked in the standard description; the name does not mean its physical nature is known.

The Hubble constant characterizes the present expansion rate. Methods for inferring it have not agreed completely: some local measurements are higher than an estimate derived from the microwave background within the standard model. This is the Hubble tension. Calibration problems, hidden systematic errors or physics beyond the current model have all been investigated; no final explanation follows from the discrepancy alone.

The large-scale expansion is well supported. Its exact present rate, acceleration mechanism and some details of its history remain active research. Uncertainty in those details does not erase the entire observational framework, just as a strong framework does not make every parameter final.

What can wa inna lamusi'un mean in context?

The verse says: “We built the heaven with strength, and indeed We are expansive” [Al-Dhariyat: 47]. Al-Tabari, Ibn Atiyya and Al-Qurtubi explain bi-aydin here as strength; it must not be confused with the plural of “hand.” Lamusi‘un comes from a root denoting breadth and can mean possessing ample capacity and power, or making something wide.

Al-Tabari explains it as having ample capacity to create the heaven and whatever God wills, and power over it, then transmits Ibn Zayd’s “He widened it.” Al-Qurtubi collects readings including being capable, possessing abundance and expanding provision, treating them as close in their indication of power and breadth. This evidence prevents two exaggerations: that all early commentators explained modern cosmic expansion, and that widening is wholly alien to the word with no basis in exegesis.

Ibn Atiyya’s reading in Al-Muharrar al-Wajiz clarifies the distinction: he allows expansive power or that God made the heaven wide, connecting the latter with Ibn Zayd. This historically supports spaciousness in the heaven’s construction, without specifying whether galaxy distances are increasing now or giving a rate or measurement method. Nor does the participle alone impose a physical process continuing to the present: the text specifies no spatial or temporal frame for measurement. Early exegesis’s lack of galaxy science also does not close the door to modern reflection. Distinguishing a possible reading from a definite identification preserves the argument’s strength.

The nearby context lists signs of power in building the heaven, spreading the earth and creating pairs, then calls people to God. Its purpose is guidance and meaning, rather than a table of observations or derivation of an equation. This does not prevent a contemporary reader from contemplating a vast or expanding cosmos; it sets the kind of inference that can be defended.

The following verses say: “We spread out the earth—how excellent are We at preparing it! And of everything We created pairs, so that you may take heed. So flee to God” [Al-Dhariyat: 48–50]. The discourse moves from heaven to earth, creation, remembrance and the call to God. Reading lamusi‘un in this setting keeps it within the verses’ purpose. Al-Tabari explains bi-aydin as strength: it does not warrant a bodily image of the Creator or a device pushing celestial objects. This interprets the text in its setting; galaxy recession is studied through observation.

How does this relate to the book's method?

The book’s name comes from: “Say, travel through the earth and observe how He began creation; then God will bring forth the later creation” [Al-Ankabut: 20]. Al-Tabari’s commentary explains that the discourse argues against those denying resurrection: as beginning creation was not beyond God, neither is restoring it. “Observe” must not be extracted as a specific command to measure expansion or advance proof of a cosmological equation. The commanded observation opens reflection on creation’s traces without dispensing with each subject’s means of knowledge.

On pages 21–24 the book describes the early hot universe, changing distances, redshift and the microwave background, then distinguishes scientific accounts of aspects of “how” from revelation's statement about Creator and purpose. This article follows that path on a specific question: start with spectra and distances, return to the verse as preserved in commentary, then assess the strength of the claimed connection.

Is the verse a scientific miracle proving expansion?

The answer requires separating four layers, following the method detailed in Quran and Science: How Do We Distinguish the Verse, Interpretation and Theory?:

  1. The text: a concise Arabic phrase can convey breadth, abundance, power or widening.
  2. Interpretation: Al-Tabari, Al-Qurtubi and Ibn Atiyya record several readings. Al-Tabari favors ample power and transmits “He widened it” from Ibn Zayd; Ibn Atiyya explains the second reading as making the heaven wide.
  3. Observation: astronomical measurements support changing large-scale distances and an expansion history.
  4. Inference: a consonance with expansion is possible; assigning Hubble's law or the acceleration rate to the verse exceeds its stated content.

If “miracle” means no collision between a broad phrase and a later observed fact, some may find a subject for reflection. If it means a specific scientific result that the verse uniquely states and could be extracted independently before observation, the linguistic and exegetical case is insufficient. Earlier scholars did not calculate the Hubble constant, redshift, dark energy or Friedmann equations from this sentence.

A useful test is whether the verse alone, without galaxy data, tells us that the distance between two distant clusters changes by a specified amount in a specified time. It does not define the objects, observers, distances or measurement. A refined acceleration model would not alter the Arabic root's meaning. One may therefore reflect on cosmic breadth as a believer without attributing to the verse a measurement it never gave.

It is stronger in faith and method not to tie revelation’s truth to a number that may change or a physical interpretation that may be revised. Science tests its models through measurement, while the verse directs attention to creation and the Creator’s power. They can meet in meaning without dissolving either into the other.

Common mistakes about expansion

  • “The Big Bang occurred at one location inside pre-existing space.” The model describes expansion from a hot, dense state, not a bomb in a cosmic room.
  • “We are the center because galaxies recede from us.” Large-scale homogeneity allows broadly similar relations for distant observers.
  • “Every galaxy recedes from every other galaxy.” Andromeda approaches the Milky Way because of local gravity, despite the overall expansion.
  • “All redshift is expansion.” Doppler motion and gravitational effects also occur; the physical setting matters.
  • “Disagreement over the Hubble constant disproves expansion.” It concerns the precise value and model, not the phenomenon's existence.
  • “The verse describes acceleration.” Lamusi'un specifies no acceleration or detailed expansion history.

Final answer

The universe expands. Its evidence comes from observed spectra, distances, supernovae, microwave background and large-scale structure. Quran 51:47 admits readings involving breadth, power and widening; it does not supply the detailed cosmological model.

We learn expansion through observations that test equations, and read the verse in its language and context concerning the heaven's construction and divine power. This lets a reader reflect on the cosmos in light of faith, while remaining able to revise scientific details as evidence improves without assigning to scripture a number it never stated.

Frequently asked questions

Is cosmic expansion a scientific fact?

Yes. The redshift–distance relationship, supernova measurements, the cosmic microwave background and their consistency within one cosmological model support it. The precise present expansion rate and dark energy’s history remain research questions.

Must “wa inna lamusi‘un” mean that the universe is expanding?

The wording can mean breadth and power, and “He widened it” is a transmitted interpretation. Al-Tabari, Al-Qurtubi and Ibn Atiyya did not formulate it as a law of increasing galaxy distances. A connection with expansion is possible reflective reading, rather than an independent linguistic proof.

Did the verse alone predict galaxy recession before observation?

The verse states that the heaven was built. Al-Tabari interpreted lamusi‘un as ample power, and a reading of making the heaven wide was transmitted. Galaxy recession and the relationship between redshift and distance were established through observation and the scientific model. We therefore should not attribute Hubble’s law or an expansion-rate measurement to the verse’s wording alone. Reflection on breadth remains a way of contemplating the verse without turning it into a specific technical anticipation.

Do galaxies and human bodies expand with the cosmos?

Not in the same way. Expansion appears between distant unbound structures; local forces dominate atoms, bodies, planetary systems and most galaxies.

Does the universe have a center from which it expands?

The standard large-scale model places no center within space from which all galaxies fly outward. Distances between remote comoving observers grow as the geometry evolves.
