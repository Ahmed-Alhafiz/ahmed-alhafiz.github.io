Does Quran 51:47 Describe an Expanding Universe?

Astronomy measures expanding distances. The verse speaks of building the heaven and breadth, with several exegetical readings.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 28 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent cosmological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Yes, the universe expands on large scales. Galaxy spectra and distances, the brightness history of supernovae, large-scale structure and the cosmic microwave background support a coherent model. Quran 51:47 says, in a close English rendering, “We built the heaven with strength, and indeed We are lamusi'un.” It does not name galaxies or their recession rate. Al-Tabari interpreted lamusi'un in terms of breadth of power and also transmitted Ibn Zayd's “He widened it”; other exegetes offered related senses. A contemporary reader may reflect on a possible resonance with cosmic expansion. Claiming that the verse unambiguously states continuous expansion or predicts Hubble's law requires stronger evidence than a shared word for breadth.

Where this appears in Qul Siru fi al-Ard
This study extends pages 21–25 of the book, which discuss expansion, the scale factor, redshift and the cosmic background, then set limits for comparisons between revelation and a scientific model. Those pages do not interpret Quran 51:47 directly. We examine it because readers of the cosmic chapter naturally ask about this verse. The manuscript starts the inquiry; commentary and observation test its general claims.

What does an expanding universe mean?

It does not mean galaxies were ejected from a central point into empty space surrounding the cosmos, nor that everything grows in the same way. General relativity describes large-scale cosmic geometry changing with time. Cosmologists use the scale factor to describe changes in distances between positions that follow the overall expansion. When it increases, two distant galaxies without a strong local gravitational bond become farther apart.

Dots on a stretching surface offer an imperfect analogy: distances between the dots increase without a center on that surface. The universe is not literally the surface of a balloon in a room, but the analogy explains why distant observers can each see others receding on average without occupying the center.

The solar system, a person and an atom do not expand with the cosmic flow. Electromagnetic bonds and local gravity hold such structures together. Expansion becomes most apparent between distant groups not bound as one local system. Andromeda, for example, approaches the Milky Way despite the large-scale trend.

On page 22 the book introduces the scale factor and a Friedmann equation. For a general reader, their role is to describe how distances change over time in a model with approximately homogeneous large-scale contents. Writing an equation does not by itself prove that a model fits our universe. Its strength is explaining several observations together and remaining open to comparison with new data. “Space expands” is a mathematical description on immense scales, not a rubbery substance pushing every nearby object. NASA explains that cosmological redshift stretches the wavelengths of light during its long journey.

How do we observe expansion without seeing space move?

Light carries a pattern of wavelengths. Astronomers compare familiar spectral lines in a distant galaxy's light with lines measured in laboratories. A shift toward longer wavelengths is called redshift; on cosmological scales the expansion during the light's journey stretches those wavelengths.

In a homogeneous model, after accounting for local motion and gravity, the ratio of observed to emitted wavelength relates to the scale factor at observation and emission:

1 + z = a(now) / a(emission)

If the scale factor doubled during the journey, the wavelength doubled too. This does not directly measure the size of the entire universe. Nor should every large redshift be translated into an ordinary speed of an object flying through a fixed background. The relation belongs to a cosmological model with specified assumptions.

Edwin Hubble's 1929 work assembled published radial-velocity measurements and galaxy-distance estimates to show an approximate relation: larger distance generally accompanied greater measured recession. The nearby-universe relation is often written as recession rate approximately equal to the Hubble parameter times distance. The early data were imprecise by today's standards, and the theory and observations had many contributors; the paper nevertheless became a landmark for a dynamic universe.

Redshift needs a distance ladder. Astronomers use calibrated variable stars, Type Ia supernovae, geometric methods and other indicators. Their confidence comes from converging observations, not from a single image or telescope. A displaced line is itself measured; estimating distance and reading the distant galaxy's redshift as expansion history require calibration and a model. ESA distinguishes cosmological stretching from an everyday Doppler shift due solely to a source moving through static space, while local velocities can also affect spectra.

ESA's Planck satellite mapped the cosmic microwave background with high precision. It does not show an “explosion” itself; the radiation was released when the early universe became transparent, hundreds of thousands of years after its hot beginning. Its fluctuation pattern helps test cosmic geometry, contents and expansion history.

Thus expansion does not hang on a single interpretation of one redshift. A model joins galaxy spectra, supernova brightness, large-scale clustering, early radiation and elemental abundances. Any revision must still explain the full pattern.

Is expansion constant or accelerating?

The rate has not remained constant across cosmic history. Matter, radiation and other components contribute differently over time. Measurements of distant supernovae in the late 1990s showed that expansion in the more recent universe accelerates rather than merely slowing under the gravity of matter. “Dark energy” is a working name for the phenomenon or component invoked in the standard description; the name does not mean its physical nature is known.

The Hubble constant characterizes the present expansion rate. Methods for inferring it have not agreed completely: some local measurements are higher than an estimate derived from the microwave background within the standard model. This is the Hubble tension. Calibration problems, hidden systematic errors or physics beyond the current model have all been investigated; no final explanation follows from the discrepancy alone.

The large-scale expansion is well supported. Its exact present rate, acceleration mechanism and some details of its history remain active research. Uncertainty in those details does not erase the entire observational framework, just as a strong framework does not make every parameter final.

What can wa inna lamusi'un mean in context?

Quran 51:47 says that the heaven was built bi-aydin and adds wa inna lamusi'un. Al-Tabari, Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi explain bi-aydin here as “with strength”; confusing it with an unqualified bodily image is unwarranted. Lamusi'un draws on breadth and abundance and can evoke ample power, making wide or providing expansively depending on the reading.

Al-Tabari explains it in terms of capacity and power to create, then reports from Ibn Zayd the sense “He widened it.” Al-Qurtubi gathers explanations including power, abundance and provision. These accounts prevent two opposite exaggerations: that all early commentators taught modern galaxy expansion, and that the language of widening has no basis at all in the tradition.

Ibn Atiyya allows an interpretation of expansive power or that God made the heaven wide, relating the latter to Ibn Zayd. This is historical evidence for a sense of spaciousness in the construction of the heaven. It does not specify whether distances between galaxies are increasing now, give a rate or describe a measurement. The participial form alone does not force a physical process continuing to the present. But the lack of modern cosmology in early exegesis does not forbid a believer from reflecting on a vast or expanding cosmos. The difference is between a possible reflection and an exclusive, demonstrable prediction.

The nearby verses describe spreading the earth, creating pairs and calling people to turn to God (51:48–50). The sequence offers signs of power and guidance rather than a table of observations. Reading “lamusi'un” in that setting keeps the verse's purpose in view while allowing a contemporary reader to contemplate the universe's scale.

How does this relate to the book's method?

The book takes its name from Quran 29:20, “Travel through the land and observe how He began creation,” followed by a reminder of the later creation. Al-Tabari understands it as an argument addressed to deniers of resurrection: the One who originated creation can repeat it. “Observe” should not be cut from this context and turned into a command solely to measure cosmological expansion or a foretelling of an equation. It does invite reflection on creation while respecting the methods appropriate to each question.

On pages 21–24 the book describes the early hot universe, changing distances, redshift and the microwave background, then distinguishes scientific accounts of aspects of “how” from revelation's statement about Creator and purpose. This article follows that path on a specific question: start with spectra and distances, return to the verse as preserved in commentary, then assess the strength of the claimed connection.

Is the verse a scientific miracle proving expansion?

Four layers must be kept distinct, as discussed in the Arabic methodological article on Quran and science:

  1. The text: a concise Arabic phrase can convey breadth, abundance, power or widening.
  2. Interpretation: al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi and Ibn Atiyya preserve more than one understanding.
  3. Observation: astronomical measurements support changing large-scale distances and an expansion history.
  4. Inference: a consonance with expansion is possible; assigning Hubble's law or the acceleration rate to the verse exceeds its stated content.

If “miracle” means no collision between a broad phrase and a later observed fact, some may find a subject for reflection. If it means a specific scientific result that the verse uniquely states and could be extracted independently before observation, the linguistic and exegetical case is insufficient. Earlier scholars did not calculate the Hubble constant, redshift, dark energy or Friedmann equations from this sentence.

A useful test is whether the verse alone, without galaxy data, tells us that the distance between two distant clusters changes by a specified amount in a specified time. It does not define the objects, observers, distances or measurement. A refined acceleration model would not alter the Arabic root's meaning. One may therefore reflect on cosmic breadth as a believer without attributing to the verse a measurement it never gave.

Neither the truth of revelation nor a scientific model is strengthened by making them depend on a precise number that may be revised. Science tests models through observation; the verse turns attention to creation and its Maker. Their questions can meet in reflection without losing their distinct standards of evidence.

Common mistakes about expansion

  • “The Big Bang occurred at one location inside pre-existing space.” The model describes expansion from a hot, dense state, not a bomb in a cosmic room.
  • “We are the center because galaxies recede from us.” Large-scale homogeneity allows broadly similar relations for distant observers.
  • “Every galaxy recedes from every other galaxy.” Locally bound systems and Andromeda's approach are exceptions to that simplistic wording.
  • “All redshift is expansion.” Doppler motion and gravitational effects also occur; the physical setting matters.
  • “Disagreement over the Hubble constant disproves expansion.” It concerns the precise value and model, not the phenomenon's existence.
  • “The verse describes acceleration.” Lamusi'un specifies no acceleration or detailed expansion history.

Final answer

The universe expands. Its evidence comes from observed spectra, distances, supernovae, microwave background and large-scale structure. Quran 51:47 admits readings involving breadth, power and widening; it does not supply the detailed cosmological model.

We learn expansion through observations that test equations, and read the verse in its language and context concerning the heaven's construction and divine power. This lets a reader reflect on the cosmos in light of faith, while remaining able to revise scientific details as evidence improves without assigning to scripture a number it never stated.

Frequently asked questions

Is cosmic expansion an established scientific fact?

Yes. Redshift and distance, supernovae, the cosmic microwave background and their consistency within a model provide strong support. The precise present expansion rate and the nature of dark energy remain research questions.

Must wa inna lamusi'un mean that galaxies are expanding?

No. Breadth and power are attested interpretive readings, while a reported sense of widening also exists. Early commentators did not formulate a law of increasing distances between galaxies. A modern connection is possible reflection, not a linguistic proof by itself.

Did the verse alone predict galaxy recession before observation?

It speaks of building the heaven and is read in classical commentary through power and breadth. Galaxy distances and the redshift relation were established through observation and models. The verse itself provides no Hubble constant or method to measure one.

Do galaxies and human bodies expand with the cosmos?

Not in the same way. Expansion appears between distant unbound structures; local forces dominate atoms, bodies, planetary systems and most galaxies.

Does the universe have a center from which it expands?

The standard large-scale model places no center within space from which all galaxies fly outward. Distances between remote comoving observers grow as the geometry evolves.
