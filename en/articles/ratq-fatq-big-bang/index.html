Ratq, fatq, and the Big Bang: text, exegesis, evidence, and limits

Does Qur’an 21:30 predict the Big Bang? The popular question collapses several distinct inquiries. This dossier separates the wording of the verse, its classical interpretation, observations, scientific modelling, and philosophical or theological inference.

By Ahmed Alhafiz — أحمد الحافظVersion 2.0Published: 1 September 2026Sources reviewed: 29 September 2026Approx. 16 minutes

The question that deserves a precise answer

The verse in Al-Anbiya begins with a powerful image: “Have those who disbelieve not considered that the heavens and the earth were joined together, then We opened them apart, and made every living thing from water? Will they not believe?” [Al-Anbiya: 30]. Modern cosmology begins with a different question: what do traces of light, elements and expanding distances tell us about the universe's past? It is easy to combine the two questions in “the Quran described the Big Bang,” but that wording is stronger than the evidence.

The short assessment: joining followed by separation is reported in early exegesis, and a believer may draw encouragement from it when contemplating a universe with a history and transformations. But commentators also recorded other meanings. Al-Tabari preferred the opening of the sky with rain and the earth with vegetation, citing the verse's continuation about water. The Big Bang is also a physical model of a hot, expanding phase, rather than an event in which we observed the heavens and the earth as two bodies separating from each other. Speaking of a possible reflective consonance is therefore honest; announcing an exact scientific identity or exegetical consensus is not.

This question lies at the heart of Part One, pp. 19–25, of “Travel Through the Earth and Observe How Creation Began”. The book presents ratq and fatq, then evidence of the early universe, and later distinguishes the verse from the equation. This article examines the connection's strength and limits: the book originates the question, while exegetical and observational sources measure its public claims.

Read the verse to its end

If “they were joined together, then We opened them apart” is isolated from the verse, attention seems directed entirely toward the universe's beginning. But the verse immediately connects it with “We made every living thing from water,” then ends with “Will they not believe?” Its surrounding context in Al-Anbiya argues from creation and governance against polytheism and heedlessness. The issue is therefore the world's indication of God's power and oneness, rather than naming one stage in an astronomical diagram.

This structure makes the rain-and-vegetation interpretation understandable: a sky that does not rain and an earth that does not grow vegetation, then rain opening the first and vegetation breaking through the second, followed by life dependent on water. The meaning of a connected heaven and earth subsequently separated is also understandable through language and some transmitted interpretations. Yet the presence of both meanings in early texts does not give them equal preference for every commentator. We should not select one and conceal another's choice.

“Have they not seen” need not mean that those addressed witnessed the world's beginning with their eyes. Commentators explain that it can mean seeing through knowledge and reflection. Even so, the word alone cannot prove that an invisible event is intended: rain and vegetation also offer visible subjects for reflection. Ibn Ashur's commentary considers visual and intellectual seeing within the verse's context.

What can ratq mean in Arabic?

Ratq denotes connection, closure and joining; fatq denotes separation, opening and splitting. The wording specifies a kind of transition, but does not alone identify whether it occurred between heaven and earth, within each of them, or in the closure of rain and vegetation. Looking up a dictionary therefore cannot replace investigating exegesis.

Ratqan is singular after the dual kanata. Al-Tabari and Al-Qurtubi explain that it is a verbal noun that can predicate a dual subject. The grammatical singular alone does not yield a statement about a “singularity” in relativity equations or a universe that was a geometric point. The rhetorical image may be powerful, but turning it into a specific physical value requires information the wording does not provide.

Early interpretations: where does preference lie?

The accounts Al-Tabari records in his text can be arranged into three main paths:

Transmitted reading What it depicts Its relation to the modern comparison
Attachment followed by separation Heaven and earth connected, then separated by God Allows broad reflection on cosmic transformation, without describing the expansion model in detail
Opening heaven and earth into layers Each was closed within itself, then differentiated A different question from “a first point that exploded”
Closure of rain and vegetation, then their opening The sky did not rain and the earth did not grow vegetation, followed by rainfall and growth Directly connected to the verse's continuation about water

The first is not an interpretation invented by modern physicists: Al-Tabari records it from Ibn Abbas, Qatada, Al-Hasan and others, and Al-Qurtubi transmits it. This matters against the claim that every meaning of joining and separation is modern. But Al-Tabari did not leave the opinions suspended: he held that the most correct reading was closure of rain and vegetation followed by rainfall and growth, explaining this through the subsequent “We made every living thing from water.” This fact cannot be bypassed when his name is cited in support of cosmological identity.

Al-Qurtubi transmitted attachment, recorded rain and vegetation, and noted Al-Tabari's preference. Ibn Ashur explained that the verse's construction can mean heaven and earth were jointly one ratq, or that each was ratq within itself, then examined the rain and vegetation supported by observation. These are not primitive cosmological equations, but efforts to interpret the discourse and its setting. Al-Tabari, Al-Qurtubi, Ibn Ashur.

What has cosmology actually established?

In its strongest supported scope, an astronomer's “Big Bang” means that the observable universe passed through a hotter, denser phase, and that expanding and cooling space left measurable traces. The popular name suggests a bomb in an empty room, but that is not the model: no explosion center has been observed from which galaxies scattered into a known pre-existing space.

The picture rests on mutually supporting evidence. Expansion: light from distant galaxies carries redshift measured alongside distances, with the relation interpreted through cosmic expansion. Microwave background: ancient light released about 380 thousand years after the hot phase began, when the universe became transparent; this is the oldest direct electromagnetic image, rather than a snapshot of “second zero.” Light elements: hydrogen, helium and deuterium abundances broadly agree with nucleosynthesis calculations for the early minutes, with remaining questions such as lithium abundance. Structure: background maps and their small fluctuations agree with later structure growth in the standard model.

These are not copies of one observation. Redshift tells us that cosmic scales changed; the background's spectrum and temperature tell us about a universe filled with hot radiation that subsequently cooled; some light-nucleus ratios test nuclear calculations long before background photons were released. Astronomers do not see the first minutes through an optical telescope. They ask whether the nuclei those minutes produced, as equations predict, remain in regions little processed by stars. These differ in the type of trace and degree of inference; they are not three photographs of one moment.

The Particle Data Group review of nucleosynthesis explains how light nuclei arise in the first minutes and how their primordial abundance is inferred from later observations. It reports broadly good agreement, alongside the unresolved lithium-7 problem and uncertainties in reactions and measurements. Mentioning the problem does not make the entire thermal history guesswork; nor should it be concealed to produce a tidy story. Deuterium abundance tests a different question from the verse of ratq and fatq: a physical calculation, rather than the identification of a Quranic word's intended meaning.

NASA explains early cosmic history and background radiation, while the final Planck publications collect observational constraints on the model. The combined evidence does not mean we have observed what preceded the hot phase, or settled inflation's mechanism, dark matter's nature or the beginning of time. For these boundaries, read What Came Before the Big Bang?.

Why is the comparison more than superimposing two pictures?

We can draw an arrow from ratq to fatq, and another from “hot, dense universe” to “expanding universe.” Similarity in a transition between states is real at this broad level. But it does not establish that the events are identical; otherwise every text mentioning joining and separation could be tied to any natural transformation.

The first difficulty: Earth. The verse mentions “earth,” while planet Earth formed far later than the first stages of cosmic expansion, about 4.5 billion years ago according to NASA. Claiming that today's Earth separated as a completed body at the universe's first moment contradicts the scientific sequence. A commentator may suggest that the name denotes creation's eventual result, Earth's material, or heaven and earth as those addressed perceived them. But this is an interpretive choice requiring independent support; it should not be passed off as a direct scientific statement.

The second difficulty: the event's nature. Many transmitted depictions of attachment present heaven and earth as two bodies subsequently separated by air. Cosmology does not formulate the first phase in this duality: it discusses changing scale factors, cosmic contents and the development of matter's structure. Replacing “air” with “space” or renaming fatq as inflation does not make the descriptions identical.

The third difficulty: alternative interpretations. Rain and vegetation have contextual strength; this reading did not originate with a modern opponent of scientific miracles, but was preferred by Al-Tabari because water is mentioned. A substantial early reading of this strength rules out claiming that the text can only mean the modern model. This does not invalidate joining and separation; it weighs the claim of exclusivity.

The fourth difficulty: what does “beginning” mean? Evidence of the hot phase does not by itself establish that it began from absolute nothingness or that physical time could not extend before it. Even a more precise account of the preceding phase would not change the linguistic principles used to interpret the verse. Knowing nothing about that phase would not make ignorance evidence that fatq equals a particular equation. Current scientific limits should not be used to elevate a Quranic interpretation, nor a model's revisability to deny what has actually been measured.

These four problems show the comparison's level: the image of a transforming world may draw the attention of someone familiar with cosmic expansion and evolution, and the separation interpretation is old. We cannot infer that the verse specified inflation's mechanism, separation's date or elemental ratios. Such additions require specific wording or an inference that excludes alternatives, neither of which is available here.

Test the strongest formulation: “The fatq mentioned in the verse is the moment when microwave background light was released after about 380 thousand years.” If this specification comes from the text, where does it mention light, transparency or that duration? Why favor light's release over the minutes when nuclei formed, or an earlier phase? If all that is meant is that the world changed state, the agreement is too broad to distinguish this discovery from other transformations. The test does not ask the Quran to be a physics textbook. It asks anyone attributing a specific physical prediction to the Quran to show how they extracted it before learning it from observation. This separates legitimate reflection from a testable claim of anticipation.

A strong objection and an honest answer

A supporter of scientific miracles may ask: how do you explain an early interpretation of attachment and separation before telescopes and the microwave background? Is this not anticipation? The answer is that the early account cannot be accused of modern invention; it indicates that the image of separation was possible through language and exegesis. But it does not make its author knowledgeable about the hot-universe model. Commentators differed about the form of ratq and fatq, and the interpretation produced no specific prediction of the microwave background, elemental ratios or Earth's later formation that could be tested before observation.

An opposing critic may argue that Al-Tabari's preference for rain and vegetation ends any link with creation's beginning. That also exceeds what the sources establish. His preference is a strongly reasoned contextual judgment, but the attachment account exists in exegesis and today's reader cannot erase it. It can be contemplated without being declared the only meaning or a technical identity. The boundary lies between possible encouragement through reflection and a required scientific belief in exact correspondence.

A deeper theological objection asks whether the verse's argumentative force is weakened if it is not an equation for the Big Bang. Consider Al-Tabari's preferred reading: rainfall and plant growth are familiar events, but familiarity and knowledge of their causes do not prevent them serving as signs of God's action in Quranic discourse. Religious meaning does not require an obscure event at observation's outermost limit. “We opened them apart” attributes the action to God, while “Will they not believe?” moves from describing the world to faith. Explaining rain's mechanism does not alone answer the question of power and governance; reconstructing cosmic thermal history does not alone establish the verse's purpose. This distinguishes kinds of question and argument, rather than a result measured by astronomical instruments.

The same honesty remains for a reader choosing attachment and separation: cosmic history can invite reflection without making certainty depend on a particular date or an initial scenario unsettled by observation. The book title's call to travel and observe stays open to human learning, rather than every revision of a cosmic phase becoming a crisis in understanding revelation.

Where does the book's path begin, and where does this study correct it?

On pp. 19–25 of “Travel Through the Earth,” Ahmed Alhafiz presents ratq and fatq as a doorway to the origin of existence, then explains expansion, Friedmann equations, the microwave background and Planck results. The book is sound in warning that the verse supplies no Friedmann equation and is not reducible to a changing model. But turning “the verse establishes a cosmic principle: ratq followed by fatq” into a published argument requires stating that locating that principle at the physical universe's beginning is the author's reading, rather than a consensus of commentators. Stating Al-Tabari's preference strengthens the argument's honesty, even if it narrows an easy formulation of “miracle.”

The title itself comes from: “Say, travel through the earth and observe how He began creation; then God will bring forth the later creation” [Al-Ankabut: 20]. Al-Tabari's commentary connects observing the first creation with arguing for the possibility of restoration and resurrection. The point is not to extract an initial temperature from the verse's words, but that observing creation bears on the argument of faith. The article can thus serve the book meaningfully: identify the inquiry, test the evidence and clearly state where observation's result ends and a believer's reflection begins.

On p. 34 the book offers a comprehensive phrase: “Travel needs a means, observation needs knowledge, knowledge needs intention, and intention needs guidance.” This path cannot be strong if an early interpretation is dropped because it fails to serve a preferred cosmological picture. Observation needs knowledge of exegesis as well as physics, and intention needs honesty in presenting both.

When the book proceeds to time and the days of creation on pp. 26–35, it applies the same principle to another question: it neither forces the six days into terrestrial hours nor assigns them to specific cosmic epochs without evidence. Defending the book's thesis therefore does not require ratq to match the “explosion” exactly. It is enough to show how science reads the cosmos and context reads the verse, then discuss theology clearly. This sequence is stronger than one scientific metaphor whose details may change.

How do we read cosmic news without reinterpreting the verse every week?

If a galaxy is observed earlier than expected, ask whether the galaxy-formation model changed, or the evidence for expansion, the background and nucleosynthesis. If a researcher proposes a bounce before the hot phase, ask whether this is a mathematical model or an observational signature that distinguishes it. Not every early-universe revision refutes the Big Bang, and not every model success adds evidence for a verse's interpretation.

The verse remains read through its language, context and transmitted exegesis, while the sky is read through its instruments and measurements. The readings may converse and inspire each other's questions, but a human interpretation receives no textual infallibility, and a scientific hypothesis no status of revelation. The book can thereby withstand changing results, rather than chasing every astronomical headline.

Result

Does early exegesis contain an image of connection followed by separation? Yes. Did Al-Tabari prefer separation at the universe's beginning? No: he preferred rain and vegetation, guided by the verse's continuation. Does the hot-universe model rest on strong evidence? Yes, with a clear limit to what it says about the beginning. But does the verse match the theory in detail and establish it before observation? Neither the language, exegetical diversity nor physical details support that claim.

This qualification does not erase the verse's value. For a believer, it points to God's power in a world neither static nor independent of its Creator, connecting creation with water, life and faith. Science opens a window on this world's history without becoming a binding interpretation of every word in the verse. This is a deeper argument than a slogan of anticipation: it lets readers test the claim rather than choose between accepting exaggeration and rejecting the text.

Frequently asked questions

Did the Quran mention the Big Bang by name or in detail?

No. The verse mentions ratq and fatq, and early exegesis includes attachment and separation, but it does not mention cosmic expansion, the microwave background or their equations.

Does rain and vegetation exclude attachment and separation?

It does not erase their presence in transmitted exegesis. But Al-Tabari preferred rain and vegetation because of “We made every living thing from water,” so the cosmological preference cannot be attributed to him.

Did Earth exist at the universe's beginning?

The planet called Earth formed billions of years after cosmic expansion began, about 4.5 billion years ago. Any literal link between the completed Earth and the first cosmic moment needs reconsideration.

Does rejecting exact identity mean the verse does not discuss creation?

No. The verse argues from creation, water and life toward faith, but identifying the physical event equivalent to fatq is not a binding conclusion of its wording.
