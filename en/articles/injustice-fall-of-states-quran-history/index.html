Does Injustice Cause States to Fall? Quran and History

Selective law, captured resources and silenced warnings can make a state fragile. History also requires attention to war, drought, institutions and reform.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 28 September 2026English edition updated: 14 October 2026Independent historical and exegetical review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Yes, injustice can weaken and help bring down states, but it is neither a sole cause nor a clock that predicts the date of collapse. When law is selective, resources are treated as spoils and reassuring misinformation is safer than truthful reports, trust, legitimacy and a state's capacity to raise revenue, coordinate and learn decline. War, food shortage, elite division or an environmental shock can then expose weaknesses accumulated earlier. The Quran places injustice, corruption and elite excess in a moral and causal frame; historical research finds outcomes depend on how shocks interact with institutions and the capacity for repair.

Where this idea appears in Qul Siru fi al-Ard
This study expands pages 74–79 and 96–104 of the book. There writing and accounting preserve rights, justice is infrastructure for cooperation, and collapse results from interacting pressures: environment, war, excess, corruption and institutional failure. This article tests that argument against tafsir and historical and institutional research.

First, what do “injustice” and “collapse” mean?

Injustice extends beyond a ruler's direct violence. It includes taking property without right, unequal treatment before courts, monopolized opportunities, underpaid labor or false measures, turning public office into private gain, and denying groups a voice, information or the means to object. It can be an individual act, or become embedded in rules that predictably produce unfair outcomes even when officeholders change.

“The fall of a state” is not synonymous with every weakness or change of government. It may mean loss of the capacity to enforce law and provide services, territorial loss, fragmentation of a political order, the end of a dynasty while society continues, or contraction of cities and trade networks. “Civilization” is broader than “state”: a political center can fall while language, religion, knowledge and people endure. These distinctions prevent centuries from being compressed into one dramatic scene.

The more precise question is not merely whether injustice preceded a fall; such co-occurrence can be found in many cases. Can we describe a causal chain from injustice to reduced ability to function and withstand a shock? Does it survive comparison with unjust systems that did not collapse quickly?

What does the Quran say, and what does it not say?

The two verses in Hud share one context: “Why were there not, among the generations before you, people of remaining virtue who forbade corruption on earth… and those who did wrong pursued the luxury they had been given,” followed by “Your Lord would not destroy towns unjustly while their people were setting things right.” The context links the absence of resistance to corruption, the pursuit of luxury and wrongdoing with destruction. Conversely, it does not merely describe people as privately good, but calls them people who set things right: society contains active resistance and correction.

Al-Tabari reported one reading of “by injustice” as associating others with God, with people still acting fairly among themselves. He also transmitted the sense that God would not destroy reforming people unjustly. Ibn Ashur interpreted the sentence against the corruption in the preceding verse: God does not wrong reforming people, while corrupters bring destruction on themselves. The range of interpretation matters. It prevents one phrase becoming a simplistic political formula, without removing fairness and reform from the passage.

The words “Indeed, We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and sent down with them the Book and the balance so that people may uphold justice” give the message a social purpose: people upholding justice. The balance is not a preaching ornament added after society is built, but a standard protecting fair dealings and rights. “God does not change what is with a people until they change what is within themselves” establishes responsibility for internal change without restricting what is “within themselves” to individual feeling: habits, rules, loyalties and institutions are made by people and in turn remake them.

The example in Quran 16:112 turns a town's security and provision into fear and hunger after ingratitude for blessings. Quran 17:16 describes elite transgression before destruction. None gives a timetable or permits us to identify every specific disaster as a particular divine punishment without revelation. Together they cultivate moral insight: blessing calls for gratitude and justice, and excess and corruption have consequences when they block repair.

Five ways injustice creates measurable fragility

1. Selective law raises the cost of cooperation. If the powerful can disregard a contract or a judge treats parties differently, general trust no longer suffices. People seek guards, intermediaries, bribes or extra guarantees, or retreat into narrower exchanges among kin. In Douglass North's account, institutions are the rules and constraints shaping incentives. Insecure rights shorten horizons and shift investment from long-term production to rents and survival.

2. Injustice erodes trust and legitimacy. Trust is not affection for government but an expectation that institutions show reasonable reliability, integrity, openness and fairness. The OECD framework describes these drivers of public trust. When people see costs imposed on them and benefits reserved for a narrow group, voluntary compliance and willingness to pay taxes or accept a hard policy during crisis decline.

3. Concentrated power damages information. Officials fear reporting failure, comforting reports are rewarded, and affected communities are kept away from decisions. The World Bank's report on governance and law shows how unequal power can foster exclusion, policy capture and clientelism, frustrating policy even where written rules look sound. A state that punishes the bearer of bad news loses its nervous system before it loses its borders.

4. Patronage and rent seeking weaken capacity. If jobs and contracts go to loyalty rather than competence, the buildings, uniforms and seals may remain while the ability to count, maintain, collect revenue and respond declines. The injustice is not only that one person was denied a right: the public lost skills and knowledge needed during a shock.

5. Unequal sacrifice breaks the common bargain. People do not require mathematically identical burdens, but they need a convincing reason to accept them. If poor families send children to war while exemptions are bought, or small taxpayers pay while the powerful evade, the state can look like an extraction machine rather than a shared undertaking. Compliance then rests increasingly on force, which is costlier and less durable than legitimacy.

From ethics to institutions: where do book and research meet?

The book's civilizational chapter treats the city as a network of material and information flows. Water, food, taxes and rights do not move alone; they need measures, records and decisions. If a register assigns a poor person's land to someone else, or their water share to a well-connected neighbor, writing that preserves memory becomes an instrument of injustice. Administrative technology is not fair by itself: its worth depends on the rules it enforces and whether they can be challenged.

This explains why a “strong state” in a coercive sense is insufficient. The ability to arrest differs from the ability to deliver water, gather truthful data, settle disputes and sustain cooperation. Research distinguishes administrative, fiscal and coercive dimensions of state capacity. An authority may have strong security forces and weak tax institutions, or sound infrastructure and dependent courts. Ask not only whether it is strong, but strong at what, for whom and under which constraints.

A well-known study by Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson presented comparative evidence linking institutional quality to long-run economic performance, using colonial conditions to estimate institutional effects. It is influential, not a final word: its instruments, measurements and conclusions have been debated. Proper use of it is not a claim that one number proves whether “justice causes a state to survive,” but recognition that rules distributing power and protecting rights are not a marginal backdrop to growth.

Why does history resist a one-cause equation?

Research on collapse compares societies across very different times, scales and records. A PNAS review warned against focusing on one stressor, such as the environment, while neglecting political, social and cultural resilience. A broad study of premodern states likewise suggests that the immediate ending may be invasion, coup or drought; the deeper question is why some states absorb such a shock while others fragment.

One drought can strike two regions: one survives because stores, distribution and information work better, while another starves after elites conceal the crisis or monopolize grain. Climate is not the opposite of an institutional explanation. It is a pressure whose exposure and response institutions shape. An external invasion may hasten the end of a state drained by privilege, yet remains a real cause in its own right; armies, resources and geography matter.

Sometimes an unjust authority lasts a long time. Resource income may fund it, expertise may be imported, opponents divided, administration effective in some sectors, or costs shifted to the periphery. Persistence does not absolve injustice, but it refutes a naive schedule. A comparatively fairer system can also fall to overwhelming invasion or a disaster beyond its capacity. History is not an instant court in which every moral outcome fully appears within one observer's lifetime.

Collapse often crosses a threshold rather than following a straight line

A state may defer maintenance, borrow, consume reserves and conceal a deficit while its surface seems stable. Every delay narrows the margin. If financial crisis coincides with divided elites and food price rises, a threshold can be crossed: confidence in currency fades, wages stop, agencies split and feedback loops accelerate.

This pattern explains why a trigger can feel sudden after a collapse. The event may be sudden; the conditions that allowed it to spread accumulated earlier. Injustice helps make those conditions by blocking cheap correction: complaints are suppressed, accountability deferred and information polished. When reform threatens the interests of those in control, the system defends the very fault that endangers it.

Four claims to reject

  • “We know the divine reason for every disaster”: without revelation one cannot identify a specific unseen purpose for a particular event; one can examine patterns and consequences without claiming such knowledge.
  • “Justice alone prevents a fall”: fairness increases resilience but cannot remove war, disease, geography or error.
  • “Survival proves an unjust ruler succeeded”: survival may be purchased by consuming the future or moving harm onto people absent from the ruler's accounts.
  • “Science has proved verses about collapse”: studies test defined variables using human measurements; they do not test revelation or exhaust Quranic injustice in an index.

How can injustice be diagnosed before collapse?

Early indicators are not prophecies, but questions: Can a harmed person reach an independent court? Are budgets and contracts public? Is a whistleblower punished while the corrupt official escapes? Does information flow from the margins to the center? Are taxes, conscription and crisis burdens distributed by known rules? Can a failed policy change without an admission becoming a threat to the whole system?

Reform here is more than an exhortation. It builds routes for objection and review, protects rights, manages conflicts of interest and publishes information so a society can correct course. The Quran's “people actively reforming” gives the act an ongoing form: social health is a capacity to detect and resist corruption, not a state achieved once for all.

How does this study strengthen the book's argument?

The book's phrase “justice is infrastructure” becomes an account that can be examined: fairness reduces transaction costs, improves information, widens participation and preserves the trust needed for resources and decisions to flow. It grows stronger when the mechanism is described as probabilistic and interdependent; readers are not promised an immediate historical punishment for every unjust actor.

This study also links the book's chapters on knowledge and civilization. Records, accounting and intelligence have no independent moral virtue apart from their purposes. A state can know its population to serve it or extract from it; an algorithm can allocate rights or entrench bias. The civilizational question is not only how much information we have, but who defines error, who can object and what the system serves.

Final answer

Injustice is a real contributor to the fall of states because it can turn wrong actions into a structure: selective law, distorted information, captured resources and exhausted trust. It does not act alone or on a schedule we know. Drought, war or disease can be the shock; the outcome is shaped by institutions' ability to perceive it, share its costs and learn.

The Quran does not invite a confident spectacle of other people's fall. It calls for those who restrain corruption and actively reform. The practical historical lesson goes beyond repeating that injustice destroys: build fairness, accountability and truthful information before the cost of correction exceeds a society's capacity.

Frequently asked questions

Does every unjust state fall quickly?

No. An unjust rule can persist through resources, repression or partly effective administration. The claim is that injustice accumulates ethical and institutional fragility, not that it fixes a mechanical date of collapse.

What does “while its people are reforming” mean?

Quran 11:117 follows the warning about corruption and elite excess. Commentators speak of God not wrongfully destroying people who actively reform, and also report an interpretation about people acting fairly among themselves. The verse establishes the value of reform, not a simple historical timetable.

How does injustice practically weaken a state?

Through selective law, lost trust, monopolized information, patronage in resource allocation, reduced willingness to cooperate and bear public costs, and disabled routes for correcting errors.

Can injustice alone explain a civilization's collapse?

No. Historical collapses often combine war, drought, epidemic, fiscal crisis or elite conflict with institutions that either amplify or absorb the shock. Injustice helps explain vulnerability and response, not every event on its own.
