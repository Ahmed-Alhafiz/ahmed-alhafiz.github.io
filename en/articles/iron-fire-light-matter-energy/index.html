Iron, Fire and Light: Matter, Energy and Justice

From star to steel, fire to engine and photons to power: understanding matter and energy also raises questions of benefit, harm and just use.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 26 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent scientific and exegetical review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Iron is an element with a cosmic history largely forged through stellar processes before it entered Earth's material. Fire is combustion that transforms chemical energy; the Sun makes heat and light by nuclear fusion; and electricity carries energy rather than being a primary source. The Quran does not present these terms as a technical lesson. It places iron alongside the Book, the balance, might and human benefit; fire within a scene of use and remembrance; and a blazing lamp within creation. Science investigates how these processes work, while revelation raises questions of purpose and justice.

Direct connection to Qul Siru fi al-Ard
This article expands the book's chapter on making use of creation, pages 131–144: iron and matter, fire and energy, lightning and electricity, the Sun and light. The subjects are gathered because they support one argument in the book: knowledge turns what exists into civilizational capability, but capability serves good only when governed by a just measure.

The connecting verse: Book, balance and iron

God says: “Indeed, We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and sent down with them the Book and the balance so that people may uphold justice. And We sent down iron, in which there is great might and benefits for people.” [Al-Hadid: 25]. The verse’s order prevents isolating iron from its purpose: among clear proof, the Book, the balance and justice comes a material carrying power and benefit.

Al-Tabari described iron's might in weapons and its benefits in people's livelihood and tools. Ibn Kathir and al-Qurtubi also discussed its uses in warfare and manufacture. Their understanding did not require stellar chemistry: one material can build and protect or kill, and its atomic structure does not decide which outcome follows. People and the institutions governing its use do.

“We sent down” should not be confined with certainty to iron falling in meteorites. The Quran uses sending down for things reaching people by God’s decree, as it says of livestock: “And He sent down for you eight pairs of livestock.” [Al-Zumar: 6]. The expression may refer to ordaining and giving, while iron’s cosmic origin remains a separate scientific fact inviting reflection, rather than the word’s only meaning.

How did iron form?

The early universe contained mainly hydrogen and helium with traces of lighter elements. Fusion inside stars built heavier nuclei. NASA describes massive stars passing through successive stages that form iron-rich cores; ordinary fusion at the iron stage no longer yields net energy, so a star can undergo collapse and a supernova explosion.

But “all iron was made by supernovae” is too simple. Observations and models point to several production channels, including explosions of massive stars and Type Ia supernovae. A sounder statement is that iron atoms predate Earth, were formed through stellar processes, and entered the cloud from which the solar system formed.

As Earth formed, much iron sank toward the core because of its density; iron compounds remained in the mantle and crust. Ore mined today is commonly found in minerals such as hematite and magnetite. It is then separated, reduced and worked into iron and steel. The U.S. Geological Survey says approximately 98% of iron ore is used in steelmaking.

The path from star to planet, ore, furnace and alloy is more informative than a slogan about iron “falling from the sky.” It is true to the scientific evidence, shows how much knowledge and work it takes to put a resource to use, and does not tie the verse to one contested lexical interpretation.

Why did iron change civilization?

Steel is not a new chemical element. It is an iron-based alloy with carbon and other elements in proportions that control hardness, toughness and corrosion resistance. Differences in microstructure and heat treatment allow one family of material to become bridges, scalpels, rails and machines.

Mining and smelting, however, use energy and generate waste and emissions; iron and steel production is a heavy industrial sector. “Benefits” cannot be measured by volume of production alone. The service life of a structure, recycling, worker safety, the effects of mine and furnace, and who controls weapons and can hold them accountable are also part of the assessment.

Fire: what happens in combustion?

God says: “Have you considered the fire you kindle? Did you bring its tree into being, or are We the ones who brought it into being? We made it a reminder and a provision for travelers.” [Al-Waqi‘ah: 71–73]. Al-Tabari explained kindling, a reminder of the fire of the hereafter, and provision as a benefit for travelers and those using it. The text joins what is observed and tangible with meaning concerning the hereafter and everyday benefit.

Combustion is a rapid oxidation reaction between fuel and an oxidizer that releases heat and often light. Energy is not a hidden substance that escapes by itself: chemical bonds are rearranged, and the lower energy of products compared with reactants appears as molecular motion and radiation. The U.S. Department of Energy explains how an internal combustion engine burns a fuel–air mixture, with expanding gases driving a piston.

In God’s words, “The One who made fire for you from the green tree” [Ya-Sin: 80], the argument concerns the power to give life: the One who brought an opposite from its opposite is capable of re-creation. Commentators explained the verse through the markh and afar trees familiar to Arabs as a means of kindling fire. Modern knowledge can add that plants store sunlight’s energy in organic compounds. This is a reflective addition, not a replacement of historical interpretation with photosynthesis.

From fire to engine: power and cost

Engines converted heat into movement at large scale, accelerating transport and production. They do not turn all chemical energy into useful work: a substantial share leaves through exhaust, cooling and friction. Combustion also yields carbon dioxide and other pollutants, depending on fuel and operating conditions.

The distinction between “it works” and “we ought to use it this way” matters. Physics can calculate efficiency and chemistry can measure emissions, but choices about who receives benefits and bears harm require values, law and accountability. Use without a just measure may transfer one generation's comfort into another's burden.

Lightning and electricity are not synonyms

Lightning is a rapid electrical discharge after charges separate within clouds or between cloud and ground. The U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration notes that the lightning channel heats air extremely quickly to roughly 30,000 degrees Celsius; violent expansion of the air produces thunder.

Household electricity, by contrast, is an organized movement of charge through a circuit under a relatively controlled voltage difference. Electrical energy is not made from nothing: a generator converts turbine movement, a solar cell converts some incoming radiation, and a battery converts chemical energy. The questions therefore include the primary source, conversion efficiency and effects.

Discovering laws does not remove danger; it allows engineers to make insulation, breakers, grounding and standards. Knowledge that is not translated into safety is ethically incomplete even if its equations are correct.

Why does the Sun shine?

God says: “And We made a blazing lamp.” [Al-Naba: 13]. The verse describes the Sun within a cosmic setting drawing attention to blessing and power. Science explains that, under immense pressure and heat, the Sun’s core fuses hydrogen into helium and converts a small amount of mass into energy. NASA gives a core temperature of about 15 million degrees Celsius, suitable for sustaining fusion.

Energy moves outward through the Sun's regions before radiating into space. A small portion reaches Earth and drives climate, photosynthesis and most energy chains used by life. Fossil fuels, too, are ancient solar energy stored by organisms and transformed over geological time.

Physical light should not be confused with the light of guidance in “God is the Light of the heavens and the earth.” A photon is a physical concept; the verse about divine light has a broader theological and figurative context. A shared word does not license turning the unseen meaning into an electromagnetic wave.

Light is an instrument of knowledge, not only illumination

When a star's spectrum is analyzed, absorption or emission lines reveal particular atoms and molecules; their displacement can indicate motion. Light allows us to see cells and galaxies, optical fibers carry information, and photovoltaic cells turn some photons into electrical current.

These examples show that using nature is not magical subjugation but discovering stable relationships and building tools that work within them. An instrument that extends sight can also extend surveillance; a communication network can carry knowledge or deception. Capacity returns us to the question of justice.

The strongest objection: are too many subjects combined?

Yes, iron, combustion, electricity and light belong to different fields; no one law explains them all. Their combination here is justified as an entry to the book's chapter on making use of creation, not as a replacement for specialized studies. The connection is the movement from observing matter and energy to transforming them technologically, then confronting two sides of the same power: might and benefit.

This article states its limit explicitly. It explains principles needed for the book's argument; it is not a comprehensive reference on astrophysics, metallurgy, combustion or electrical power engineering.

Final assessment

Does iron's stellar origin by itself establish a “scientific miracle” in “We sent down”? No. The phrase can convey bestowal or provision, and iron's cosmic history is more complex than meteorite falls. The fact can prompt sound reflection if presented as a fact about the material mentioned, not as the only possible lexical meaning.

Does science compete with the meaning of making use of creation? Understanding the complex path from star to steel and from photon to solar cell shows how a resource becomes usable only within laws, material limits and consequences. The book's strength here is not a prediction of modern terminology but the placement of power and benefit under the Book, balance and justice.

Frequently asked questions

Did all the iron on Earth come from meteorites?

No. Its material has a cosmic origin before Earth and entered the planet's formation. Iron meteorites also arrive, but Earth's ores cannot all be attributed to them.

Is fire a substance?

A flame is a region of combustion reactions with hot gases and particles emitting light. Fire names the phenomenon, not a separate chemical element.

Does the Sun burn like wood?

No. Wood undergoes a chemical reaction with oxygen; the Sun's energy comes principally from nuclear fusion in its core.

Is electricity a primary energy source?

It is a carrier or form of energy produced from different primary sources. We need to know its source to judge its effects.

Does “Light of the heavens and earth” mean physical light?

The verse cannot be reduced to photons: its theological and figurative context is broader than a physical term.
