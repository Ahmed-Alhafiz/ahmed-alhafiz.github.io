How Do Concepts Form? Names and Models

From a word to a testable model: classification, prediction and the limits of knowledge.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 30 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent cognitive-scientific and exegetical review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Two people can point to the same water. One gives it a name; the other asks why ice floats, how water works in cells, and how its presence is inferred elsewhere. Naming is useful but does not contain those answers. A concept forms as we treat different things as sharing relevant features or relations and can use the grouping to reason, predict, act and revise mistakes. A word may direct attention and help transmit a category, but classification does not all begin with language and a label does not guarantee a true category. A scientific model connects concepts through specified relationships, assumptions and measurements that can be tested, without becoming a complete copy of reality.

This study expands pages 39–43 of Ahmed Alhafiz's Qul Siru fi al-Ard, from name to concept, relationship and scientific model. Quran 2:30–33 supplies a reflection on teaching and the limits of created knowledge, not a laboratory equation about cognition.

What does the passage about Adam's names say?

In Quran 2:30–33, God tells the angels of a successor on Earth, their question about corruption and bloodshed appears, and God says He knows what they do not. God then teaches Adam the names, presents them, and the angels acknowledge that they know only what He has taught them. Adam reports the names. The passage does not conclude that knowledge prevents a human from wrongdoing. Teaching appears within a larger question about capability and responsibility.

Commentators differ about the scope of “the names.” Al Tabari reports views about names of things, angels and Adam's descendants, favoring descendants and angels in part because of the pronoun in “presented them,” while allowing a wider Arabic possibility. Al Qurtubi discusses names, naming and named things, including kinds and uses. Al Razi raises the possibility that learning encompasses qualities and properties, not merely sounds; that is his exegetical argument, not a consensus that the verse describes modern experimental classification. Ibn Ashur considers both the teaching of particular names and the enabling of Adam to name and express thoughts, relating communicative ability to teaching others and the accumulation of knowledge. The verse does not specify exactly how the teaching happened or demonstrate that Adam held a theory of every named item's properties.

We cannot extract from the word “names” alone a chronology of language origins, Adam's brain anatomy or the periodic table. The book reflects on a taught, accountable human and on how later people investigate. Quran 16:78 reminds people that they emerge from their mothers knowing nothing and are given hearing, sight and minds; al Tabari reads it as a reminder of the gift of later learning, not a laboratory map of cognition.

Name, category, concept and model

A name is an expression used to point or communicate. A category groups things by a distinction; a concept enables us to use such a distinction in thought and inference. Philosophers disagree about their exact boundaries. A child may know “bird” but initially treat every flying creature as a bird, including bats while excluding ostriches. Learning more changes which features matter, and what the category predicts about anatomy, reproduction and relatedness.

Categories can be useful for one purpose and misleading for another. Sun and lamp are both light sources in one comparison, but their energy mechanisms differ. Good conceptual work states which property is being measured, when a grouping helps and which case would make us revise it. Seeing a bat fly and an ostrich unable to fly can prompt a child to rethink the first rule. That is an illustrative scenario, not a report on a particular child's experiment.

A scientific model is a selective representation of a phenomenon: a table, diagram, equation or simulation specifying elements, relationships and conditions so it can explain, predict or compare. Its simplification is often deliberate. Evaluate a model by its domain, assumptions, measurements, alternatives and what it predicts beyond the data used to fit it. A broader theory may support several models; some models are exploratory or predictive without being full causal explanations. A successful map is not the entire terrain.

Water offers the book's progression: a common name, measurable properties, then models that connect molecular structure with solubility and temperature. H₂O written on a page is not the water in a cup, and the formula alone does not predict everything a cell does with it.

Does a concept require a spoken word?

No. Infant research reports discrimination and generalization before children can pronounce category names. But a looking-time result by itself does not reveal an adult-rich inner concept, and task design affects what can be inferred. Studies of new visual categories by Althaus and Mareschal found that a shared spoken label could direct roughly one-year-old infants' attention toward common features. That does not mean a sound pours an essence into a mind; it may change attention and help a pattern be extracted. In a different comparison, visible motion helped category learning more than a label. Effects depend on the task and experience.

Nor is all classification uniquely human. Pigeons trained to distinguish images of birds and mammals generalized to some new drawings and mixed forms; researchers also found that visual orientation could explain much of their behavior. This does not show that a pigeon understands biological ancestry as a zoologist does. It does prevent the facile claim that animals cannot discriminate kinds. The book's image of a human naming water is best read as a starting point for the wider human capacity to explain, measure, write, criticize and transmit knowledge across generations.

When does classification predict something new?

Scientists knew many elements and scattered properties before the modern periodic table. Mendeleev was not the first to arrange them, but his table left spaces and predicted features of unknown elements from neighboring patterns. The Royal Society of Chemistry discusses his “eka-aluminium” and the later discovery of gallium in 1875, as well as scandium and germanium. In its historical comparison, an approximate predicted atomic weight of 68 stands against gallium's 69.72, and predicted solid density 6.0 g/cm³ against a value around 5.9. These are particular successes, not proof that every prediction was exact.

Early organization relied on atomic masses and known properties, with difficult cases. Later work established atomic number as the basis of the modern ordering. The successful prediction retains its value, while the explanation of why the pattern holds was improved. A gap in a beautiful table is not enough; the test is whether previously unknown things have properties close to those specified in advance. Failures call for revisiting measurement and assumptions, not continually changing words to shield a scheme.

How do we know something we cannot touch?

A star can first be named as a bright point. A spectrograph separates its light by wavelength; emission or absorption features can be compared with laboratory measurements of matter. Along with models of temperature and density, the pattern supports inferences about elements in an atmosphere, motion and other properties. NASA explains that elements have characteristic spectral lines. An astronomer does not touch a star, but does not infer its composition merely from the name either.

Here are four levels: “star” is a name; its measured spectrum is observation; matching a pattern to atomic lines under specified conditions is an inference; estimates of composition and motion belong to a physical model. A single colored image without calibration is insufficient. The periodic table and starlight illustrate human inquiry, not a claim that Quran 2:31 predicted either discovery.

Three errors in the path to knowledge

Naming as explaining: a label can make a condition easier to discuss without proving its cause or that every case grouped under it is the same. Ask what is measured and what counterexample would change the group.

Linguistic similarity as scientific discovery: an ancient expression resembling a modern term does not carry its instruments, error margins and experimental history. Reflection on creation is legitimate; attributing a precise mechanism to a verse needs independent interpretive evidence.

A successful model as a complete reality: a model can work well in a known range and need modification elsewhere. A human statement of a law describes and tests regularity; it is not every detail of the world itself.

Why does responsibility matter?

In Adam's story the angels say that they know only what God taught them (Quran 2:32). A believer may draw humility from it without pretending it specifies an experimental protocol. Quran 17:36 cautions against following what one does not know and invokes hearing, sight and heart or mind as accountable; al Tabari includes not claiming to have seen or heard what one has not. That ethical sense matters when someone reports a result.

Authors should identify which statement is an observation, plausible interpretation, open hypothesis, or quotation from scripture or a book. A model used to make decisions affecting people also raises questions about assumptions, errors and consequences. The book connects knowledge in its second part to entrusted responsibility, then writing and social justice. A grand label should not conceal uncertainty or merge unlike people into one unreviewed judgment.

Frequently asked questions

What is concept formation?

Developing distinctions that group cases and support inference, prediction or action, with the ability to revise them when counterexamples appear.

Does a word create an idea?

Not in general. Nonverbal categorization exists, while words can direct attention and aid teaching in some circumstances.

Does Adam's teaching of names describe modern cognitive science?

No. It belongs to the Quranic account of teaching, knowledge and responsibility; applying cognitive studies to reflection is a later comparison.

How is a concept different from a model?

“Element” can specify a kind; a periodic-table model organizes relations between elements and can yield testable predictions.

Is a scientific model the same as a theory?

They can relate, but a broad theory may support several bounded models. There is no fixed ladder or identity between the two.

Did Mendeleev's prediction make his entire table infallible?

No. It supported a particular pattern; later atomic-number ordering improved its explanation.

Why connect knowledge and responsibility?

Naming, measuring and using things can help or harm. The account of Adam's teaching does not remove the surrounding question of what humans do with knowledge.

Limits of this edition

Independent author-led research, not a peer-reviewed academic paper. Published studies and commentary were reviewed internally. External specialists in exegesis and cognitive science have not yet reviewed this synthesis.

Evidence transparency

Scripture and exegesis ground historical interpretive claims; experiments concern bounded tasks in infants and animals; scientific history concerns testable predictions. The four-step schema is the author's analytical teaching device, not a universal law of cognition.
