Light and Nur in the Quran: Sun and Moon

Visible moonlight is reflected sunlight. Quranic words for light reach beyond one physics definition.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 30 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent astronomical, theological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Quran 10:5 calls the Sun diyaʾ and the Moon nur, while speaking of lunar stations, years and calculation. Physics shows that the Sun emits energy generated in its interior and that visible moonlight is reflected sunlight. Yet the two Arabic words cannot be frozen into a rule that diyaʾ always means self-produced light and nur always means reflected light. Commentators discussed intensity, context and wider usage. “God is the Light of the heavens and the earth” in Quran 24:35 concerns a divine statement, parable and guidance; God is not a collection of photons.

Why ask about light and nur?

A moonlit path and a bright midday landscape are familiar but invite different questions: where does the light come from, how does the Moon appear luminous, and what does Quran 10:5 itself say? Pages 140–143 of Ahmed Alhafiz's Qul Siru fi al-Ard move from the Sun's radiation and spectrum through photons, silicon and solar cells, then distinguish created physical light from the scriptural language of guidance in Quran 24:35. We can read the verse in its Arabic and exegetical setting, and describe the phenomenon with physics, without making either a copy of the other.

What did commentators say about Sun and Moon?

Quran 10:5 does not stop at naming illumination: it says that lunar stations make counting years possible and that creation has a true purpose. Al Tabari explains the Sun's daylight and the Moon's night illumination and discusses the Moon's stations. His interpretation does not assign a photon-generation mechanism to each word.

Ibn Ashur calls diyaʾ strong, bright light and nur a broader term that can apply to weak or strong rays. This accommodates the different intensity and uses of Sun and Moon. In Quran 71:16, the Moon is called a light and the Sun a lamp. Ibn Ashur treats the lamp as a comparison suited to familiar experience and suggests that “Moon as light” hints at the reflection of solar rays. His observation matters, but it does not make “reflected light” the exclusive meaning of nur in every Quranic verse. The physical claim rests on observations of Sun and Moon, not on a forced dictionary rule.

Quran 25:61 again mentions a lamp and an illuminating Moon, and Quran 78:13 calls the lamp intensely shining. These expressions draw on observation, gratitude and imagery. A lamp is not a claim that the Sun burns oil; its intensity is not an equation for nuclear fusion.

Where do sunlight and visible moonlight come from?

The Sun is a star. Nuclear fusion in its interior converts hydrogen into helium and releases energy. Energy moves through solar layers, and a range of electromagnetic radiation eventually leaves the surface. The Moon absorbs some incident sunlight and scatters or reflects some toward us. NASA explains that visible moonlight is reflected sunlight. Its surface is relatively dark, reflecting roughly a tenth of incident light on average; proximity and the dark sky make it look bright to Earth observers. It is neither a perfect mirror nor a self-luminous visible lamp.

One qualification prevents overstatement: a warm Moon also emits thermal infrared radiation. Thus “the Moon produces no light of its own” should be limited to the visible moonlight in question, not all electromagnetic radiation at every wavelength.

What changes through the lunar month?

Sunlight illuminates about half the Moon at a time. As the Moon moves around Earth, we see varying fractions of that illuminated half, from crescent to quarter and full Moon and back. Earth's shadow causes a lunar eclipse only under a particular alignment; it is not the cause of ordinary monthly phases. Orbital inclination helps explain why every full Moon is not an eclipse.

The repeated pattern makes the verse's stations and calculation more than an aside. A lunar month from one new Moon to the next averages about 29.5 days; a circuit relative to distant stars is about 27.3 days. Earth moves along its orbit during that circuit, so returning to the same Sun–Earth–Moon alignment takes longer. These are scientific averages for related but distinct periods, not hidden numerical codes in the word “stations.” Observing and recording the Moon's appearance over successive nights can test part of this explanation.

What is light in physics?

Visible light is a portion of the electromagnetic spectrum, alongside infrared, ultraviolet, radio waves and more. A photon's energy depends on frequency, E = hf, and in vacuum frequency and wavelength satisfy c = λf. A violet photon generally carries more energy than a red one, but perceived brightness also depends on photon count, spectrum, eye sensitivity and viewing conditions. Spectroscopy uses interactions of radiation with matter to infer properties of distant objects.

These equations describe measurable created radiation. They can help explain spectra, fibers or solar cells. They cannot simply be moved to every use of “light” in Arabic or the Quran. “The lamp illuminated the room” concerns measurable radiation; “the idea illuminated the problem” describes understanding through a metaphor. Context selects the kind of question and the appropriate tool.

“God is the Light of the heavens and the earth”

Quran 24:35 presents a niche, lamp, glass and oil within an extended parable and speaks explicitly of guidance and God's examples. Al Tabari discusses several interpretations and favors one concerning Quranic guidance in a believer's heart; Ibn Ashur distinguishes senses of light within the verse and its context. Quran 42:52 also calls revelation a light by which people are guided. This is not light whose wavelength a spectrometer measures.

Matters concerning God's names and attributes deserve care. The passage is respected as stated, and commentators have differing approaches to its explanation. The relevant limit for this essay is that physical electromagnetic radiation is part of the created world. God is not a wave, photon or luminous body with laboratory parameters. Recognizing guidance as a prominent aspect of the verse does not mean that all theological questions about the divine attribute are settled by a physics analogy.

Did the wording predict reflected moonlight?

The astronomical fact is sound: visible moonlight is reflected sunlight. The rigid lexical argument that Sun=diyaʾ always means internally produced and Moon=nur always means reflected is not sound. Al Tabari describes their effects in day and night; Ibn Ashur says nur is broader in 10:5 and sees a hint of reflection in 71:16. Text, exegetical inference and physical evidence are three different levels. The use of nur for revelation and guidance alone defeats a universal rule about reflection.

Source, surface, medium and observer

White and black cloth under one lamp appear different because materials absorb and return different portions of incident light. The Moon looks bright in a dark sky although its surface is fairly dark. Near the horizon the Sun's color changes because of atmospheric scattering, not a change in the fusion mechanism inside it. A spectrometer separates wavelengths and examines absorption and emission features, making inferences about elements, temperatures and motion through tested models. Light carries measurable traces of matter; it does not by itself tell us how to use that knowledge ethically.

From sunlight to a device

A solar cell absorbs photons suited to its semiconductor's energy bandgap; resulting charge carriers are separated and collected as electric current. It does not convert every photon without loss. The US Department of Energy explains efficiency and its limits. Pages 140–142 of the book link a photon, an electron and a hole to current and then the wider use of silicon and electronic circuits. The point is not that Quran 10:5 describes a transistor. It is that understanding physical laws can produce tools, and people remain responsible for how benefits and costs are distributed.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between diyaʾ and nur?

In Quran 10:5 they distinguish Sun and Moon. Ibn Ashur describes diyaʾ as strong illumination and nur as broader. Usage and context prevent one fixed physical definition for every verse.

Does the Quran explicitly say that the Moon reflects sunlight?

It does not state a technical reflection mechanism in those words. Astronomy establishes the visible phenomenon, and Ibn Ashur suggested an implication in his reading of Quran 71:16.

Does the Moon emit any radiation?

It emits thermal infrared radiation, although its visible moonlight is overwhelmingly reflected sunlight.

Are Moon phases caused by Earth's shadow?

Ordinary phases show how much of the sunlit half we see as the Moon orbits. Earth's shadow is involved in an eclipse.

Does “blazing lamp” prove nuclear fusion from the word alone?

No. It conveys a bright, hot Sun in familiar imagery. Fusion is supported by astrophysical evidence, not by the word “lamp” alone.

Is God a form of physical light?

No. The created electromagnetic spectrum is a subject of physics; Quran 24:35 calls for contextual theological interpretation, with guidance and parable prominent.

Why does the book connect light and silicon?

It traces the use of measurable radiation in semiconductors and asks how that knowledge becomes beneficial, responsible action.
