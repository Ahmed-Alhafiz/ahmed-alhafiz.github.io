Quran and Science: How to Test a Comparison

A practical method for testing comparisons between the Quran and scientific claims.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 26 September 2026English edition updated: 14 October 2026Independent scientific and exegetical review: not yet completed

Short answer

We should not begin with a modern scientific theory and search the Quran for a word resembling it. Nor should changes in scientific knowledge lead us to reject every comparison between revelation and the cosmos. A disciplined method first distinguishes five things: the Quranic text, human interpretation of the text, observation or measurement, the scientific model explaining the data, and the philosophical or theological conclusion a person draws.

Once these are distinguished, we can say precisely: this is a meaning the verse can bear; this is a reading offered by commentators; this is a strong observational result; this is a well-supported scientific model; and this is a further inference that the laboratory alone does not establish. Combining these layers into a statement such as “science has definitively proved the verse” turns the comparison from an argument into a weakness.

The central conclusion of this article is: the Quran commands observation, but does not exempt us from the principles of interpretation; science reveals some mechanisms of creation, but cannot by itself determine its purpose and ultimate destiny.

The article’s connection to the book
This is not an unrelated topic attached to Qul Siru fi al-Ard fa-Unzuru Kayfa Bada’a al-Khalq (“Say, Travel Through the Earth and See How Creation Began”). It directly expands the book’s introduction, especially pages 7–17. There Ahmed Alhafiz explains that science studies phenomena and regularities, while revelation addresses origin, purpose and ultimate destiny. He warns against imposing changing theories on the Quran or turning religious discourse into preaching that fears questions. This article turns that view into a testable method to be applied throughout the research series.

First: What does the verse “Say, travel through the earth” command?

God says:

“Say, travel through the earth and see how He began creation. Then God will bring about the final creation. Indeed, God has power over all things.” [Al-Ankabut: 20].

It is a mistake to detach the opening of the verse from its ending. The verse does not stop at how creation began; it moves from that question to the final creation and God’s power to resurrect. This is the central meaning al-Tabari discusses: those who deny resurrection are invited to consider the beginning of creation, since the One who first brought it into being is not incapable of bringing it back.

Al-Qurtubi adds consideration of the abundance of creatures and their differing forms, languages, colors and natures, and of the dwellings and remains of past nations. Ibn Ashur explains that familiarity with one’s surroundings can distract a person from reflection. The command to travel therefore invites observation of the traces of creation and the succession of nations, and inference from the beginning to the possibility of re-creation.

These readings share three meanings:

  1. Observation is commanded; asking about creation is not a departure from faith.
  2. The field of observation is broad: creatures, humanity, the remains of nations, and the succession of life and death.
  3. The verse’s purpose concerns belief and the hereafter: inferring re-creation and complete power from the beginning.

The commentators did not, however, say that the verse contains the names of theories that would appear centuries later, or that every result in physics and biology directly interprets it. Modern science can expand our knowledge of the scenes we contemplate, but does not permit us to replace interpretation with an equation or a microscopic image.

Second: The Quran is a book of guidance—does that prevent scientific inquiry?

Calling the Quran a book of guidance does not mean that it forbids studying the world; the verse itself commands travel and observation. It means that the text’s highest purpose is not to teach the details of physics, chemistry and embryology as specialist textbooks do.

This distinction preserves two positions together:

  • We do not reduce the Quran to an encyclopedia of natural sciences from which every discovery awaits extraction.
  • Nor do we separate guidance from the world until cosmic verses become mere rhetorical images with no bearing on reason and inquiry.

Al-Shatibi criticized attributing all the sciences of earlier and later generations to the Quran, regarding much of this as an excessive claim: the Quran was revealed in Arabic and within the familiar conventions of its discourse. This warning does not prevent benefiting from modern knowledge in reflection. It prevents claiming that every scientific term lies within a Quranic word even when language and context do not support it.

The rule is: science may explain a phenomenon to which a verse refers, but cannot create a meaning that its words could not bear.

Third: What science are we comparing with the verse?

“Science” is sometimes used as though it were a single body of final truths. That is inaccurate. The US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine’s report on reproducibility describes science as a mode of inquiry that asks questions about the world and evaluates answers and degrees of certainty through collective work seeking to establish them on evidence.

It identifies four general aims of scientific work:

  1. Describing phenomena.
  2. Explaining them.
  3. Predicting what will happen.
  4. Intervening in processes or systems.

There is no single sequence of steps followed identically by every science. An astronomer does not recreate a star’s formation in a laboratory, but collects light and measurements and tests models through independent predictions. An embryologist combines observation, imaging and experiment. An archaeologist builds an inference from layers, inscriptions, dating and context.

Despite different tools, the sciences share reliance on evidence, disclosure of method, peer scrutiny, assessment of uncertainty, and the possibility of correction when stronger data emerge.

A scientific theory is not a guess

In everyday speech, “theory” can mean an uncertain idea. In science, a theory is an explanatory framework supported by a substantial body of knowledge and evidence and subject to testing and revision. A theory therefore does not become a “law” when established: a law describes a pattern or relationship, whereas a theory explains why and how that pattern occurs within its domain.

The strength of the word “theory” does not make every detail final. A general principle may be firmly established while a particular mechanism, numerical value or early stage remains under investigation. Judgment concerns the specific claim and its evidence, not its name alone.

Fourth: Five layers that must not be confused

1. The Quranic text

The words, structure and context of the verse and its relation to what precedes and follows it. The Quran’s transmission is certain, but not every meaning attributed to its words is unambiguous.

2. Interpretation

Human effort to understand the text through language, context, transmitted accounts and scholarly principles. It has a recognized standing, but is not equivalent to revelation. More than one valid reading may fit the wording, while a reading may be weak because its evidence is inadequate.

3. Observation and measurement

Examples include measuring the shift in light from galaxies, imaging a stage of embryonic development, or dating an archaeological layer. Even measurement has instruments, limitations and a margin of error, but differs from the account we construct to explain it.

4. A scientific model or theory

A framework connecting and explaining multiple data sets and generating testable predictions. Its strength grows with the diversity and independence of evidence and its ability to pass tests, rather than repeated media coverage.

5. Philosophical or theological inference

For example, one person says, “The existence of a natural law rules out a creator,” while another says, “This laboratory result alone proves every fundamental principle of faith.” Both move beyond describing a phenomenon to a metaphysical judgment requiring further premises.

Science can say that a phenomenon follows a measurable relationship. Whether the world is self-sufficient or created with wisdom does not follow from a measuring instrument alone: these are judgments on a philosophical and theological level.

Fifth: Evidence does not all have the same standing

Putting a link after a sentence is not enough. We must know the type of source, whether it actually supports the claim, and how strong it is.

LevelExampleWhat does it establish?
Original text or direct dataA verse, a hadith with its chain of transmission, a measurements table, a published inscriptionEstablishes the existence of the text or data, not the validity of every interpretation of them
Original peer-reviewed studyAn experiment or data analysis with a disclosed methodSupports a specific result within a sample, conditions and limitations
Systematic review or specialist consensusA review combining multiple studies or an academic reportPresents the weight of evidence and the wider state of knowledge
Institutional explanatory sourceA university or scientific agency pageUseful for explanation, but not always a substitute for original research
News or popular contentA newspaper, video or postIntroduces a subject, but alone is insufficient to establish the claim

In science, revision does not mean that everything could be overturned tomorrow. The National Academies report explains that scientific knowledge is robust and revisable at the same time: some results have survived repeated tests, while their limits and details remain open to improvement. Correction is part of science’s method, not a scandal.

Sixth: Four degrees of comparison between a verse and science

Not every correspondence is a “miracle.” Comparisons can be arranged in four degrees:

First degree: no contradiction

The sound meaning of a verse does not conflict with an established scientific result. This matters, but does not mean that the verse supplied the theory itself.

Second degree: compatibility or illumination

Modern knowledge reveals depth in a scene the verse invites us to observe, such as water’s central role in life. Reflection deepens, but we must not attribute details to the wording that it did not state.

Third degree: specific correspondence

The text has a specific linguistic meaning preceding the discovery, and a strong independent scientific result corresponds to it beyond general resemblance. This is a larger claim requiring rigorous linguistic, exegetical and historical examination.

Fourth degree: a demonstrative miracle claim

Its proponent claims that the text supplied precise knowledge unavailable at the time of revelation and admitting no other natural explanation. This is the highest degree and carries the heaviest burden of proof. An attractive image, flexible translation or contemporary statement made after the discovery is insufficient.

Much discourse about “scientific miracles” jumps from the first or second degree to the fourth without establishing the evidence.

Seventh: Testing a claim through eight questions

Before publishing “science has proved this verse,” answer the following questions:

  1. What is the full verse and its context?
    A claim is not built on an isolated word.

  2. What can the Arabic actually mean?
    Consult usage and language, not a resemblance in sound to a foreign term.

  3. What did commentators say before the theory appeared?
    A modern reading may be legitimate reflection, but must not be presented as an original, agreed meaning if it is not.

  4. What is the precise scientific claim?
    Do not compare the verse to a broad word such as “energy” or “universe,” but to a result that can be formulated and examined.

  5. How strong is the scientific evidence, and when does it date from?
    Is it a repeated measurement, a broad review, a preliminary study, or a hypothesis circulating in the media?

  6. Are we comparing the text with data or an interpretation of data?
    Observation, model and philosophical inference are different things.

  7. Are there competing linguistic or scientific explanations?
    Hiding alternatives does not strengthen the argument; it makes it collapse at the first review.

  8. What could change our judgment?
    If no evidence is allowed to alter the connection, we are protecting it from testing rather than testing it.

The outcome is not limited to “true” and “false.” A connection may be strong, probable, possible, weak or forced. Stating its degree is part of intellectual honesty.

Eighth: A quick application to two examples

The example of ratq and fatq

The verse in Al-Anbiya mentions the joining and parting of the heavens and earth, then makes water the basis of every living thing’s life. Disciplined inquiry starts with the meaning of ratq and fatq, the context of the argument, and commentators’ readings, then presents the evidence for the hot cosmological model. If it finds general compatibility, it says so without claiming that fatq contains the equations of relativity or every stage of cosmic history.

The example of embryonic stages

The verses in Al-Mu’minun describe successive stages. Linguistic inquiry examines nutfah, alaqah and mudghah, the sequence in context, and interpretive readings, then compares them with modern embryology. A Quranic term must not be replaced with a modern microscopic name and the two proclaimed identical in every respect. Nor should the Quranic sequence be ignored because science uses a different technical classification.

In this way, the next article applies the method rather than repeating an earlier slogan.

Ninth: Can science prove or disprove the Quran?

Scientific inquiry can examine claims about the material and historical world when formulated in a testable way. Its results may strengthen or weaken a reading, reveal an error in information a writer attributes to the text, or show a striking correspondence between a valid meaning and a discovered reality.

Experimental science does not test every matter addressed by revelation: it does not put resurrection, angels or the ultimate purpose of existence in a test tube. A matter of the unseen being beyond experiment does not refute it, just as faith in it is not a laboratory result.

Three judgments must therefore be distinguished:

  • The text is true: a judgment of faith resting on the source of revelation and its overall evidence.
  • This is a sound interpretation of the text: an exegetical judgment resting on language, context and interpretive principles.
  • This is a strong scientific result: a specialist judgment resting on evidence and method within the scientific domain.

If a connection proves mistaken, we must review our interpretation, scientific understanding or both, rather than attribute the error to the Quran and then defend it in religion’s name.

Tenth: Intention is not a substitute for method

The book’s introduction opens the path with the report of Umar ibn al-Khattab, may God be pleased with him: “Actions are only according to intentions, and each person has only what they intended.” It is recorded in both Sahih collections: al-Bukhari includes it at the start of his collection, and Muslim records it as number 1907.

Intention governs the goal: do we seek truth or victory? Do we want to guide the reader or impress them? It does not make weak evidence strong. A person may intend well yet misread language, repeat a false report or overstate a preliminary result. Inquiry therefore needs sincerity and method together.

Conversely, a researcher may have precise tools and use them for arrogance, deception or harm. Qul Siru fi al-Ard therefore does not separate knowledge from trustworthiness: science expands human capability, while revelation reminds people of their responsibility for its direction.

The charter for the Qul Siru fi al-Ard series

From this article onward, the series commits to these rules:

  1. Do not attribute to the Quran a meaning unsupported by its wording and context.
  2. Do not present one commentator’s position as a consensus.
  3. Do not cite a hadith before checking its source and grading.
  4. Do not call a preliminary hypothesis an established fact.
  5. Do not reduce science to a newspaper page or sensational report.
  6. Do not conceal genuine disagreement in interpretation or a specialist field.
  7. State the degree of each claim and the limits of its supporting source.
  8. Distinguish scientific results from philosophical conclusions built on them.
  9. Correct the article publicly if stronger evidence appears.
  10. Return each study to the series’ purpose: travel becomes observation, observation becomes insight, and knowledge becomes responsibility.

Final answer

How do we distinguish a verse’s meaning from a scientific theory?

Begin with the text and its context, review language and interpretation, then identify the scientific claim, its source and degree of certainty. Distinguish data from model and philosophical inference. If the verse can bear the meaning, the scientific evidence is strong, and alternatives and limits are not concealed, comparison is justified to the extent that both sides establish it. But changing a word’s meaning after every discovery, or treating general resemblance as final proof, moves from reflection to forced interpretation.

The stronger method does not fear science or use the Quran as its decoration. It honors revelation, respects reason and gives evidence its due weight, leaving the verse its greater purpose: leading people from observing the beginning of creation to faith in divine power, ultimate destiny and responsibility.
