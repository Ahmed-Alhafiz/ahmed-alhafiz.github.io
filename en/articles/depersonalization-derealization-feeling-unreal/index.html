Why Do I Feel Unreal or Like I’m in a Dream? Depersonalization and Derealization Explained

Feeling unreal, detached from your body, or as if the world is dreamlike can be frightening. This guide explains depersonalization and derealization (DPDR), what can cause similar experiences, what treatment evidence shows, and when to seek help.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished: 7 October 2026Source review: 7 October 2026 · Independent specialist review: pending

Short answer

If you feel unreal, as if you are watching yourself from outside, or as if the world around you is dreamlike, foggy, distant, or behind glass, the experience may be described as depersonalization or derealization. A brief episode does not by itself mean you have depersonalization/derealization disorder. Diagnosis depends on the pattern, distress, effect on daily life, and whether other causes need to be excluded.[1][2]

When the word “unreal” may hide an emergency

This page is about depersonalization and derealization, but sudden confusion is different. If someone suddenly cannot think or speak clearly, does not know where they are, has a new seizure, new weakness, serious injury, suicidal intent, or immediate danger to self or others, seek urgent medical help rather than assuming the experience is DPDR.[10]

Depersonalization vs derealization: what is the difference?

Depersonalization is detachment from yourself: your body, thoughts, emotions, voice, actions, or sense of being the person living your life may feel unfamiliar or distant. People may describe feeling robotic, numb, or like an outside observer of themselves.[1]

Derealization is detachment from your surroundings. Familiar people, rooms, streets, sounds, or objects may feel unreal, flat, foggy, artificial, unusually sharp, far away, or dreamlike—even while you know that the world has not literally changed.[2]

ExperienceCommon descriptionWhat feels unfamiliar?
Depersonalization“I feel detached from myself” or “I’m watching myself from outside.”Your self, body, thoughts, emotions, or actions.
Derealization“Everything feels unreal” or “It feels like I’m in a dream.”Your surroundings, people, places, time, distance, or atmosphere.

The two can occur separately or together. The American Psychiatric Association groups both within dissociative disorders while distinguishing them from dissociative amnesia and dissociative identity disorder.[3]

Why do I feel unreal or like I’m in a dream?

“Why do I feel unreal?” is a useful starting question, but the sensation is not a diagnosis by itself. Depersonalization and derealization can appear during severe stress, anxiety or panic, trauma-related conditions, depression, other dissociative problems, substance use, sleep disruption, and some neurological or medical situations. A 2026 scoping review found DPDR symptoms across a wide range of clinical and non-clinical contexts, reinforcing why one symptom should not be assigned one cause without context.[6]

Mayo Clinic lists severe stress, fear, trauma, anxiety, depression, and certain drugs among factors associated with depersonalization/derealization experiences, while also noting that the cause of the disorder is not fully understood.[2] That distinction matters: an association is not proof that the same cause applies to every person.

If you are searching phrases such as “I feel like I’m in a dream,” “I feel detached from reality,” or “I don’t feel real,” describe the exact experience to a clinician rather than forcing it into a label. When did it start? Is it constant or episodic? What was happening with sleep, panic, substances, medications, illness, or stress? What other symptoms came with it? Those details can matter more than the name you found online.

Can anxiety or panic cause derealization?

Depersonalization and derealization can occur with anxiety and panic, and clinical literature has repeatedly described that overlap.[6] But “I was anxious” does not automatically prove that anxiety is the whole explanation. New, persistent, severe, or functionally impairing symptoms deserve assessment—especially when the onset follows substance use, a medication change, a neurological event, major sleep loss, or another significant change.

This is also why reassurance alone may not solve the problem. A person can know intellectually that a room is real while still experiencing it as profoundly strange or distant. The sensation itself can then provoke more fear and repeated checking, which may intensify distress even when the person’s reality testing remains intact.

Does derealization mean I’m losing my mind?

One of the most frightening parts of DPDR is the thought: “Am I losing my mind?” In depersonalization/derealization disorder, people typically retain reality testing: they recognize that the feeling of unreality is a feeling rather than proof that reality has literally changed.[1]

That does not mean you should diagnose yourself by this distinction, and it does not mean every person with psychosis lacks all insight. The safer approach is to tell a clinician both what the experience feels like and what you believe is actually happening. Differential diagnosis belongs to a full clinical assessment, not to a single online symptom checklist.

Can seizures or neurological conditions cause similar experiences?

Yes, some neurological events can produce depersonalization- or derealization-like phenomena. In a 2019 retrospective study of patients with focal epilepsy evaluated at one presurgical center, nine patients with depersonalization-like experiences and seven with derealization-like experiences were compared with 28 control patients who had other experiential seizure phenomena.[4]

Depersonalization-like symptoms in that sample were more often associated with frontal epileptic zones, while derealization-like symptoms were associated with temporal lobe epilepsy. The study was small, retrospective, and drawn from a specialized epilepsy population; it does not tell an ordinary reader how likely epilepsy is and does not turn a description of unreality into a seizure test.[4]

The practical lesson is narrower: similar subjective experiences can arise in different contexts. If symptoms are new, unusual, accompanied by loss of awareness, seizures, neurological changes, or other concerning signs, professional evaluation matters.

How do you stop depersonalization or derealization?

Searches for “how to stop derealization” or “how to stop depersonalization” often come from people who want the sensation gone immediately. There is no single technique that can responsibly be promised to stop every episode. Treatment depends on whether a person has the disorder itself, another condition causing similar symptoms, or a broader pattern involving anxiety, trauma, depression, sleep, substances, or another medical issue.

Mayo Clinic describes talk therapy as the main treatment for depersonalization/derealization disorder and notes that medication may be used for associated depression or anxiety; no medication has been proven specifically to treat the disorder itself.[11]

A feasibility randomized trial published online in December 2025 and in the 2026 journal volume tested a cognitive-behavioral therapy adapted for depersonalization/derealization disorder. Thirty participants were randomized, and 19 completed the final assessment. The study reported an encouraging signal but was designed to test whether a larger trial was feasible, not to establish definitive treatment superiority.[5]

So the useful question is not simply “Which trick stops DPDR?” but “What is maintaining this experience in my case, what else needs ruling out, and what treatment plan fits that pattern?” Do not stop prescribed medication or start a new treatment solely because of something you read here.

When should I seek help for feeling unreal?

Seek a professional assessment if the feeling keeps returning, causes significant distress, interferes with work, study, relationships, sleep, or daily functioning, or makes you avoid normal activities.[2] It can help to write down when episodes occur, how long they last, what else you notice, current medications or substances, recent sleep changes, and the questions you want to ask.

If the experience comes with sudden confusion, a new seizure, fainting, severe neurological symptoms, suicidal intent, or immediate danger, use urgent or emergency care rather than waiting for a routine appointment.[10]

Frequently asked questions

Why does everything feel unreal but I know it is real?

That description is compatible with derealization, where surroundings feel unreal or dreamlike while reality testing is usually preserved. It is still not enough to diagnose the disorder or identify the cause.[1]

Why do I feel detached from my body?

Detachment from your body, thoughts, emotions, or actions can be described as depersonalization. Similar language can also appear in other conditions, so the full context matters.[3]

Can derealization last for days or months?

Episodes can be brief or prolonged. MSD notes that symptoms may last hours or days, or persist for weeks, months, or longer in some people. Duration alone does not determine the cause.[1]

Is depersonalization the same as multiple personalities?

No. Depersonalization/derealization disorder and dissociative identity disorder are distinct diagnoses within the broader dissociative-disorder category.[3]

Can cannabis or other drugs trigger derealization?

Substances can be associated with depersonalization or derealization experiences, and substance history is part of the clinical context clinicians may need to consider.[2]

Is derealization psychosis?

They are not the same concept. In depersonalization/derealization disorder, reality testing is generally intact. But an online article cannot determine whether a particular experience is DPDR, psychosis, a neurological condition, a substance effect, or something else.[1]

Maryam in Umm Abbas: who gets to define the experience?

In Ahmed Alhafiz’s forthcoming psychological-horror novel Umm Abbas, Maryam experiences a disturbing distance from herself and from what is happening around her. The concept of depersonalization can help a reader notice that distance, but it does not diagnose the fictional character or turn the novel into clinical evidence.

The literary question is about more than symptoms. When Maryam tries to describe what she is experiencing, other people around her also compete to name it. That creates a second layer of fear: not only “What is happening to me?” but “Who has the authority to decide what my experience means?”

This is where the research and the novel meet without being confused. Clinical sources help define what depersonalization and derealization can mean in real life. The novel uses ambiguity to explore what happens when a frightened family treats interpretation as certainty.

Spiritual interpretation, uncertainty, and care

For Muslim readers, belief in the unseen does not remove the duty to be careful about claims made about a particular person. Qur’an 17:36 warns against following what one does not have knowledge of; applying that principle here is an ethical argument for evidential restraint, not a claim that the verse is a medical description of DPDR.[7]

A hadith narrated from Usamah ibn Sharik contains the instruction to seek treatment. The citation used here records the grading shown on the source page.[8] The point is limited: seeking care is compatible with religious belief. Neither a strange feeling nor a religious interpretation should be used to justify coercion, stopping treatment, or claiming certainty about an individual cause without evidence.
