Why Do I Do Things I Don’t Remember? Memory Gaps Explained

When evidence of an action remains but the memory is missing: a medical and religious reading of Maryam’s experience and the “Dark Margin,” separating clues from explanations.

By Ahmed Alhafiz — أحمد الحافظPublished: 1 October 2026English source review: 11 October 2026 · Independent specialist review: not yet completed

Short answer

People can do things they later do not remember in different circumstances, but a trace on a phone does not by itself establish who performed the action or why the memory is missing. Assessment begins with timing, context and what other people actually witnessed, with urgent medical help needed for sudden confusion. A memory gap alone does not prove possession or any particular disorder.[1][2][5]

This is general educational material by the author, not a medical diagnosis or a religious ruling for an individual case. Independent external medical and religious specialist review has not yet been completed.

When the trace of an action remains but its memory is gone

You find a message sent from your account, or someone tells you that you spoke with them, but you cannot recover what happened. The question goes beyond forgetting a name or appointment: how can there be evidence of an action I do not remember performing?

This is the problem that confronts Maryam in the chapter “The University Laughs” in Ahmed Alhafiz’s novel «أم عباس لجلب الحبيب وردّ المطلّقة». Mocking messages and a photograph of a talisman appear from her account, then she finds photographs and a short video on her phone. Yet she does not remember filming or sending them. On pages 71–79, the fear shifts from something outside her to doubt about her relationship to her own actions.

A single story cannot diagnose the cause of a memory gap. The NHS memory-loss guidance notes that repeated forgetting that affects daily life warrants assessment; memory problems can be associated with stress, anxiety, depression or sleep disturbance, alongside other causes. The useful question begins by describing the experience, then investigating its cause.

What do we mean by a memory gap?

“Memory gap” describes an experience; it is not the name of one specific disease. Were details of a conversation missing, or hours of the day? Does it happen repeatedly? And is the belief that an action occurred based on a witness, or only on a file found on a phone?

These questions separate what we know from what we infer. A message appearing in someone’s account establishes that it appeared there; by itself, it does not establish who sent it or that person’s state of awareness at the time. This is a distinction about evaluating evidence, not a medical diagnosis and not an accusation that an account was hacked.

In the novel, the additional photographs and Maryam’s fear make the scene more convincing as fiction. But narrative plausibility does not turn a fictional event into a clinical record. A reader can empathize with Maryam without deciding the cause of what is happening to her.

Sleep, dissociation and medical causes: different possibilities

Actions during sleep

According to the NHS sleepwalking guidance, some people may get up or carry out activities without being fully awake and may not remember them the next morning. Factors associated with episodes can include sleep deprivation, stress and some medications; recurrence or risk of injury warrants medical advice.

This supports the possibility that behavior can occur with limited later recall in some sleep disorders. But it does not prove that every nighttime message was sent during sleep, or that the fictional scene represents one specific disorder. That distinction matters when reading the “Dark Margin” on pages 79–80, where possibilities involving disturbances of awareness and sleep are raised.

What does the brain study add—and what does it not settle?

Mainieri and colleagues in 2025 compared 15 adults with disorders of arousal from sleep with 15 healthy participants. They performed resting-state functional imaging and magnetic resonance spectroscopy while participants were instructed to remain awake, and they recorded the patients’ sleep for two nights with video and polysomnography. The patients had been diagnosed before the imaging.[7]

They found differences in connectivity involving the sensorimotor network and posterior cingulate cortex, as well as in glutamine measured in those regions. Associations between imaging findings and clinical or psychological measures did not survive correction for multiple comparisons.[7]

The study adds a question about how movement and awareness are coordinated. The sample was small and came from a single center; resting-state imaging does not capture an unknown act or a missing memory at the moment it occurs, and it does not become a test for diagnosing the reader or disproving a supernatural explanation.[7]

We do not diagnose Maryam or the reader because the question sounds similar.

Dissociative amnesia

The NHS guide to dissociative disorders explains that some conditions can involve feeling detached from oneself or the surroundings, or being unable to recall personal information and events. Dissociative amnesia differs from ordinary forgetting, and assessment requires ruling out other medical causes. Dissociation can be associated with highly stressful experiences, but one memory gap does not establish trauma or dissociative identity disorder.

So it is not valid to jump from “I don’t remember” to “I have multiple personalities.” Describing one’s own actions as feeling unfamiliar is not a complete diagnosis either. Similarity between everyday language and clinical language can open a question for assessment; it does not make competing explanations equally supported.

Is “dissociative fugue” a name for every action we do not remember?

The MSD Manual Professional explains that dissociative fugue is an uncommon form of dissociative amnesia associated with travel or wandering away from one’s usual environment together with loss of autobiographical memory. The term is not a synonym for everyday absent-mindedness or every message a person does not remember sending. Diagnosing dissociative amnesia requires assessing its impact and excluding other causes, including seizures, medications and head injury.[8]

This distinction helps when reading the “Dark Margin,” pp. 79–80: the margin opens several possibilities for understanding Maryam’s disturbance, but terms need definitions before they are applied. The photographs, ink mark and emotions described in the chapter do not, by themselves, diagnose dissociative fugue.

Likewise, the use of the word “dissociation” to describe differences between sleep and wake states should not be merged with the psychiatric diagnosis of a dissociative disorder. Sleepwalking research addresses one possible mechanism in disorders of arousal; psychiatric diagnosis addresses a clinical pattern with its own criteria. Sharing a word does not prove that the mechanism and the diagnosis are the same thing.[7][8]

Some neurological seizures can affect memory for the event

MedlinePlus on focal seizures explains that some seizures impair awareness and that the person may not remember what happened during them. A fixed stare or repetitive movements may be observed. Those features are worth reporting to a clinician if they occur, but they do not make every memory gap an epileptic seizure and they do not diagnose Maryam from a fictional scene.[9]

The page also notes that brain activity can appear normal after a seizure or between seizures; one normal EEG therefore does not settle every question. Clinicians interpret test results together with the event description and context. A negative or normal result in one test does not, by itself, become evidence for possession.[9]

Other causes require assessment

The MedlinePlus medical encyclopedia lists multiple causes of memory loss, including head injury, some neurological diseases, medications and substances, and some vitamin deficiencies. Timing of onset, recurrence and accompanying symptoms help guide assessment. A clinician may choose tests according to the case; not every test is needed for every person.

Listing several possibilities does not mean the person has the most serious one. Likewise, seeing bipolar disorder on a general list does not make an embarrassing action or memory lapse evidence of bipolar disorder. Diagnosis requires a broader picture than one event and should not be reduced to a ready-made explanation from family members or the internet.

What does «أم عباس» add to our understanding of the experience?

The horror in chapter four is social as well. Maryam’s name spreads among the students, and the trace left on the phone becomes material for ridicule before she understands what happened. Other people possess a quick explanation; she possesses fear and questions. The narrative shows how humiliation can outrun care.

On page 73, Maryam’s face does not appear in the short clip, and the narrative gives no conclusive evidence about who recorded it. On page 77, the ink stain near her thumb brings the question back to the body. The clues work together to increase doubt; no single clue becomes an independent answer. This is an analysis of how the scene is constructed, not a forensic examination of the phone.

Most importantly, Maryam’s fear shifts from “What happened?” to “Can I trust myself?” When family members or the university reduce the experience to a ready-made label, the character becomes more isolated inside the story. Rana’s response therefore matters: she does not have a final explanation, but she recognizes the suffering before the cause is settled.

Rana does not solve the mystery. Her value lies in stopping the jokes once she understands the fear and encouraging her friend to seek help. Dr. Qais al-Dalil, meanwhile, asks on pages 115–116 for sleep, events and witnesses to be recorded while observation is kept separate from interpretation. This narrative choice shifts attention from proving an accusation to understanding what is happening to Maryam.

For a reader trying to organize a similar experience for a medical appointment, it can help to prepare notes:

Information should be gathered with the person’s consent where possible, without publishing their recordings or turning their life into humiliating surveillance.

Do not let the question plant the answer

The MSD Manual warns about suggestion during attempts to recover memories and about accepting recovered material as accurate without external verification.[8] That is why it is more useful to ask a witness, “What did you see?” than “Did you see something controlling her?” Do not force the person to fill the gap with an explanation that satisfies the family.

This is how I read Qais al-Dalil’s position in the novel: when he separates recording from interpretation, he protects the question from other people’s desire to settle it. “I don’t know” can be the honest answer for now while assessment continues, rather than manufacturing a memory or accusation.

Does memory loss prove possession?

Memory loss by itself does not establish a demonic cause. Islamic belief in the existence of jinn has textual foundations, including Surah al-Jinn, verse 1; attributing one particular event to possession is a separate claim that requires its own evidence, and a memory gap alone does not provide that evidence.

The “Dark Margin” in the novel places popular descriptions beside medical possibilities and leaves Maryam with a question about control over her actions. That comparison can be discussed while respecting religious belief and still recognizing that medical causes are not excluded by impression. An unknown cause does not become known merely because a supernatural explanation is selected.

What about the verse, “Satan made me forget to mention it”?

In the story of Moses and his young companion, the companion says: “None made me forget to mention it except Satan.” The context of Qur’an 18:60–64 and Ibn Kathir’s commentary concern the fish and forgetting to mention what happened at the rock.[10] A discussion of the Islamic perspective should not simply erase the text’s attribution of forgetting to Satan.

But inference has limits: the verse does not provide a diagnostic criterion for dissociative amnesia and does not say that every person who forgets an action is possessed. This distinguishes the meaning of the text from applying it to a particular case; it does not deny what the text affirms. In Maryam’s case, we would first need to verify the act and who performed it, then establish whether memory was absent and why, before attributing the event to a specific explanation.

Verification and responsibility: two questions fear does not settle

The Qur’an says, “Do not follow what you have no knowledge of,” and al-Sa‘di explains the verse in terms of verification and not following what one does not know.[11] Applied here, an incomplete clue should not become an accusation of lying or a categorical claim of possession. Asking for evidence is part of disciplined religious reasoning while acknowledging what remains unknown.

The hadith of the Pen being lifted includes the phrase “from the sleeper until he wakes.” The report appears in Sunan Abi Dawud 4398; the source page displays al-Albani’s grading as authentic, and al-Dorar al-Saniyyah reports Ibn Kathir’s judgment that one narration has an isnad meeting Muslim’s conditions.[12] That judgment about the chain does not mean the hadith itself appears in Sahih Muslim.

The hadith establishes that the state of sleep matters to responsibility, but it does not prove that an unknown act occurred during sleep. Nor does merely saying “I don’t remember” create a blanket ruling that responsibility is absent. The medical question about why recall is missing and the religious question about responsibility for a particular act both require facts about that event; neither is answered by the stigma the university directs at Maryam.

At the end of the chapter, Maryam imagines two terrifying possibilities: that she did something she cannot remember, or that something is using her. This is a powerful formulation of psychological horror; outside the novel, however, it does not require assessment to be limited to those two choices. Attribution of the act itself may still be uncertain, and missing memory can have different explanations. Protecting the question from a premature answer serves the novel’s value without turning it into evidence for a supernatural cause.

A person may choose prayer or safe religious support alongside medical care. Fear does not justify stopping treatment, coercing the person, or ignoring symptoms that require help.

When does help become urgent?

The NHS guidance on sudden confusion states that sudden onset of confusion needs urgent medical help, particularly when speech is affected or the person does not know where they are. Some causes can be serious and treatable. Contact the local emergency service; the phone numbers listed by the NHS apply to the United Kingdom.

While waiting for help, stay with the person, reassure them with short sentences, and gather medication information if possible. Do not turn the moment into an interrogation or an attempt to prove possession. If memory problems are gradual or recurrent without an emergency, arrange a medical assessment. Do not stop prescribed medication on your own.

Questions readers ask

Can someone do something and later not remember it? Yes. That can happen in different circumstances, including some sleep or memory disorders; the description alone does not identify the cause.

Does missing memory mean the person is lying? Lack of recall does not prove lying, just as a person’s account alone may not settle every detail. The information needs calm evaluation.

Can possession or dissociative fugue be diagnosed from symptoms alone? These descriptions do not determine the cause. Medical diagnoses have criteria and require assessment, while general religious evidence does not establish the cause of one individual event.

Is Maryam a documented medical case? Maryam is a fictional character. Her scenes help us think about fear, stigma and uncertainty; they do not provide a diagnosis for a real person.

«أم عباس» is read here as a novel about someone who finds herself accused before she finds an explanation. Knowing the limits of the evidence gives the reader room for empathy and help-seeking and prevents the experience from being reduced to a single verdict.
