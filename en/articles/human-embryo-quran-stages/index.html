Embryonic Stages in the Quran and Modern Science

The Quran describes stages; embryology traces overlapping processes. Compare them without forcing a microscopic match.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 26 September 2026English edition updated: 14 October 2026Independent embryological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Human beings did not witness their own beginning. They did not choose their first cell, set it dividing, or draw the position of their heart or the course of their nerves. They began in their mother's womb before possessing memory or will, then emerged able to ask: How did I begin?

The Quran answers in concise language: a nutfah, then an alaqah, then a mudghah, then bones clothed with flesh, then another creation. Embryology reveals an immensely complex sequence of fertilization, division, implantation, differentiation and organ formation. The two descriptions meet in a central truth: a human being does not appear complete all at once, but develops through stages.

This does not mean that every Quranic word is a modern laboratory term, or that science has confirmed every widespread interpretation of the verses. The hardest example concerns bones and muscles: embryonic formation overlaps; it is not the completion of an entire skeleton followed by the appearance of muscles.

The conclusion is: conflict between the Quran and embryology is not inevitable, but a strong defense of the verses begins by discarding exaggerations, rather than concealing objections. This is the method Ahmed Alhafiz adopts in “Travel Through the Earth and Observe How Creation Began”, pp. 106–115.

The short answer

  • Nutfah: a small reproductive beginning; it is not an exclusive name for the sperm cell.
  • Alaqah: early commentators interpreted it as blood or a piece of it, while its linguistic root also includes attachment. It should not be restricted to a leech or made into a laboratory term.
  • Mudghah: a small stage taking shape; visual resemblance to a chewed morsel is a possible observation, not independent proof.
  • Bones and flesh: the skeleton and muscles develop through overlapping, interacting pathways; the verse does not say that all ossification is completed before any muscle tissue begins.
  • Another creation: commentators offer several interpretations, the best known being the breathing of the soul. A laboratory cannot measure or identify the soul.

Why were embryonic stages mentioned?

God Most High says:

“We created the human being from an extract of clay. Then We placed him as a nutfah in a secure resting place. Then We created the nutfah into an alaqah, and created the alaqah into a mudghah, and created the mudghah into bones, and clothed the bones with flesh; then We brought him forth as another creation” [Al-Muminun: 12–14].

In Surah Al-Hajj, the context begins with “If you are in doubt about resurrection,” then mentions the nutfah, alaqah and mudghah [Al-Hajj: 5]. After the stages in Surah Al-Muminun come death and resurrection [Al-Muminun: 15–16].

The verses therefore have a purpose wider than anatomy: the first creation is evidence of power, and a reminder that human beings did not make themselves. Science can describe development; resurrection is a report about the unseen and a theological argument, not a laboratory experiment.

This is the principle the book formulates on p. 108: science explains the stages and mechanisms, while revelation places them within creation, determination, trial and resurrection. Turning the verses into a medical textbook narrows their meaning; separating them from the real body empties their argument.

Nutfah: a small beginning carrying the body's unfolding plan

In Arabic, nutfah means a small amount of water, and was used for the beginning of reproduction. Al-Tabari interpreted the “secure resting place” as the womb prepared for the nutfah to settle.[1]

Science distinguishes several events within this beginning: the union of the gametes, formation of the fertilized cell, early divisions, the morula, the blastocyst, and implantation. Nutfah therefore should not be identified with the sperm cell alone, nor exclusively with the zygote: these scientific classifications are narrower than the Quranic word.

The image's force lies in the contrast, rather than the discovery of a hidden microscopic name: an entire body begins from an exceedingly small amount. On p. 109 of “Travel Through the Earth,” the author develops this meaning: the beginning is small, yet carries genetic material, signals and timing. Smallness here is not emptiness; it is an order whose form has not yet unfolded.

“A secure resting place”: the womb is not a passive container

A fertilized cell alone is not enough for a pregnancy to continue. The blastocyst must reach the uterus at an appropriate time, interact with its lining and begin implantation; tissues then develop that contribute to the placenta and the living relationship with the mother's body. Reviews of early human development describe this period in terms of communication, organization and the gradual specification of cell fates.[2]

The verse does not explain hormones or reproductive immunology, but describes the core reality: a prepared place of settlement. The book expresses this precisely on p. 110: the womb is not a passive container, but a complex biological environment. Science thus deepens our understanding of “resting place” without forcing its details into the word.

Alaqah: what did commentators say, and what does implantation reveal?

Honesty must come before excitement here. Al-Tabari interpreted alaqah as “a piece of blood,” and mudghah as a piece of flesh.[1] We should therefore not claim that early commentators unanimously understood implantation or the shape of a leech.

Yet the root ʿ-l-q also includes adhesion and clinging. After implantation, the pregnancy attaches to the uterine lining rather than remaining free within the cavity, and an increasingly close biological relationship with the mother's body begins.

A precise formulation is:

Alaqah has a linguistic and exegetical range broader than a single scientific term. A connection can be observed between attachment and implantation without claiming that the word is a modern microscopic designation.

This prevents two errors at once: the embryo is not a blood clot, and the older exegetical understanding should not be erased to manufacture a modern correspondence. On p. 111 the book takes the same position: the word is not equated with a particular laboratory term, but opens reflection on attachment and transformation.

Mudghah: a description of a stage taking shape, not a publicity image

Mudghah is a small piece of a size that can be chewed. At an early stage, repeated structures called somites appear along both sides of the embryo's axis. Some of their derivatives later contribute to parts of the vertebrae, muscles and skin.[3][4]

Some embryo images may resemble a chewed morsel, but the resemblance depends on the angle, embryonic age and presentation. One image is therefore not decisive proof.

The stronger meaning is that mudghah describes a stage between the initial simplicity and the visible human form: a small mass that has begun differentiating but is incomplete. This is the focus of p. 112 of the book: organs do not appear all at once; axes, layers, buds and tissues begin, heading toward different fates.

Science does not see separate “boxes”

Embryologists use the Carnegie stages, a system of 23 stages describing approximately the first 60 days after fertilization. Classification relies more on morphological features than age alone, because developmental timing naturally varies, and the tables themselves do not entirely agree on the ages of some stages.[5]

A “stage” is therefore a descriptive point within continuous development, not a wall at which all processes stop. Implantation, formation of the embryonic layers, appearance of somites and the beginnings of organs follow one another and overlap.

This is a fundamental difference between the text and the slogan: the text says creation proceeds through stages, while the weak slogan turns stages into rigid schedules and announces a correspondence day by day.

The strongest objection: do bones form before muscles?

God Most High says: “We created the mudghah into bones, and clothed the bones with flesh.” Some readers may understand this to mean that a fully ossified skeleton is completed first, and muscles only begin once it is finished.

That understanding is scientifically incorrect. Much of the skeleton begins as cartilage templates gradually replaced by bone, while skeletal and muscle lineages and tendons arise within an interacting system. Muscle forces later influence the shaping of the skeletal structure itself.[6][7] Muscles also do not appear all at once: they undergo specification, differentiation, fusion and prolonged maturation.[4][8]

Does this establish a contradiction? Not necessarily, because the verse did not say:

  • That all bones had been completed or mineralized.
  • That no first muscle cell appeared before completion of the skeleton.
  • That the two processes are separate, with no overlap.
  • That this transition occurred on a specified embryonic day.

The verse describes a visible structural transition: bones, then a covering of flesh. “Clothing” describes the skeleton's relationship to the flesh surrounding and connected to it; it does not require denying overlap in the microscopic beginnings.

But we must not weaken the text with the wrong defense. We should not say, “Science has proved that completed bones appear first, followed by muscles.” Rather, we should say:

The skeleton and muscles develop through overlapping pathways; a bodily organization then appears in which the skeleton provides support and muscles clothe it. The verse describes the transformation and structural relationship, rather than each tissue's first cellular moment.

Here the book's value is particularly clear. On p. 113, the author explicitly says that bones and muscles arise “through pathways overlapping in time and space,” rejecting the interpretation that all bones are completed before separate muscles arrive. This candor does not weaken the argument; it prevents it collapsing before the first embryology textbook.

“Then We brought him forth as another creation”: more than one interpretation

Al-Tabari preferred the interpretation that “another creation” is the breathing of the soul, but reported other opinions: human conditions after birth, the growth of hair and teeth, and attainment of mature youth.[9] Al-Baghawi and Al-Qurtubi reported similar diversity; Al-Qurtubi held that the wording encompasses speech, cognition and other transformations.[10]

It is therefore wrong to claim unanimous agreement on one interpretation, or to identify the breathing of the soul through an X-ray or a gene. Science can measure growth, movement and biological activity, but has no instrument for measuring the soul as something belonging to the unseen.

On p. 115 the book places the expression within two transitions: increasing bodily integration, then a question extending beyond anatomy to cognition, will, language and responsibility. This does not mean biology has proved the soul; rather, human beings cannot be reduced to a list of tissues when the question concerns moral obligation and meaning.

Why do weeks differ between sources?

Post-fertilization age begins at fertilization, while clinical gestational age usually begins on the first day of the last menstrual period: about two weeks before fertilization in a typical cycle. The same event may therefore be called the third week after fertilization or the fifth week of pregnancy.[5]

Any diagram assigning a Quranic word to a definite week without explaining the counting method offers an illusion of precision.

Are these verses a scientific miracle?

If this means that they describe human development through stages, from a tiny beginning settling in the womb to an organized body, this is a sound meaning whose significance deepens with knowledge.

If it means that each word is a technical term allowing only one modern discovery, and that every microscopic image matches it literally, the claim exceeds both the text and science.

The Quran does not need us to conceal the interpretation of alaqah, deny the overlap of bones and muscles, or turn the soul into a phenomenon that can be imaged. An honest defense of the Quran is part of defending it.

What does “Travel Through the Earth” add?

The book does not present the embryo as an isolated “miraculous fact”; it makes it the beginning of the path through Part Four:

An embryo taking shape ← a brain perceiving ← water sustaining life ← matter, energy and information put to use ← a human being responsible for that use.

Its distinctive contribution in the embryo chapter rests on four things:

  1. It begins with reflection on oneself, rather than the pursuit of curiosities.
  2. It explains division, implantation, somites and differentiation in accessible language.
  3. It explicitly states the limits of correspondence between the verse and the laboratory.
  4. It connects the first creation to resurrection and responsibility, rather than information alone.

This article therefore does not repeat the book, but opens its most difficult doorway and tests its method against the best-known objections. For connected reading, consult “The Human Being Within: The Embryo, the Brain and the Limits of the Soul”, pp. 106–120, then the methodological article: How Do We Distinguish a Verse's Meaning from a Scientific Theory?.

Conclusion

The Quran and embryology meet in the reality of gradual formation, a tiny beginning, settlement in the womb and transformation into an organized body. But the Quran does not use laboratory language, and embryology does not measure resurrection, the soul or purpose.

Alaqah is not a single laboratory term, mudghah is not a publicity image, and bones and muscles do not form as two separate completed pieces. Nevertheless, contradiction is not inevitable: the verse describes the structural transition and visible relationship, rather than the cellular timetable some defenders have added to it.

Here the article reaches the heart of “Travel Through the Earth”: science reveals how human beings take shape, while revelation asks what they do with this knowledge. Someone whose beginning was beyond their will, and who grew through laws they did not establish, should not end their journey at knowing the mechanism; that knowledge should begin their awareness of their limits and responsibility.

Frequently asked questions

Does alaqah mean a leech?

It should not be restricted to that meaning. Al-Tabari interpreted it as a piece of blood, while its root carries the meaning of attachment. A connection to implantation can be observed, but there is no exclusive scientific correspondence.

Is the embryo at the alaqah stage a clot?

No. It is a living, organized cellular structure. The older exegetical expression should be understood in its linguistic context, rather than as a modern medical definition.

Are bones completed before muscles?

No. Skeletal and muscle pathways overlap, and many parts of the skeleton pass through a cartilage stage before ossification. The verse does not state that all bones are complete before the first muscle tissue.

Does science identify when the soul is breathed in?

No. Science measures functions and tissues, and has no instrument for measuring the soul.

Does embryology prove resurrection?

It does not prove it experimentally. The Quran argues from the first creation for the Creator's power to bring it about again.
