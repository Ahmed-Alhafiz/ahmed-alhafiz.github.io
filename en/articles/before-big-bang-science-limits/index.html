What Happened Before the Big Bang? The Limits of Science and the Question of Creation

We know a great deal about a hot universe that expanded and cooled, but the word “before” takes us to the boundary where direct observation ends and competing models begin. Precision here is not an evasion; it is central to scientific method.

By Ahmed Alhafiz — أحمد الحافظPublished: 28 September 2026Sources reviewed: 29 September 2026Independent cosmology review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Science does not yet have a definitive empirical answer to what was “before the Big Bang.” The evidence strongly supports that the observable universe was once hotter and denser, then expanded and cooled. But it does not turn a hypothetical mathematical moment at t=0 into an observed event, nor does it prove that nature emerged from philosophical “nothing.” At Planck energies, describing gravity is expected to require a quantum theory that has not been settled. Different models propose a boundary before which time has no meaning, a contracting phase ending in a bounce, or a quantum origin; none has won decisive observational support.

What astronomers mean by “the Big Bang”

The name is slightly misleading. It was not matter exploding from a single center into a pre-existing empty space, but an expansion of distances in a universe that was hotter and denser. As we run the equations of an expanding universe backward, temperature and density rise. Several independent lines of evidence converge: galaxy redshift, the cosmic microwave background, the abundance of light elements, and the formation of large-scale structure. The “early hot universe” is therefore a strong result, not a lone speculative story.

If you ask what we actually saw and what we inferred, the distinction matters. The cosmic microwave background is light released about 380,000 years after the hot phase began, when the universe became transparent; it is not an image of a zero second. The proportions of hydrogen, helium, and other light elements test the physics of the first minutes, while galaxy redshift reveals expansion in later cosmic history. The convergence of evidence strengthens the thermal model, but it does not turn eras from which no photons reach us directly into eyewitness observation. NASA’s timeline of cosmic history makes the distinction between trace and event clear.

The strength of this part does not automatically transfer to everything said about the first moment. When we use general relativity to extrapolate toward Planck time, roughly 10⁻⁴³ seconds on the conventional scale, we enter a regime where gravity is expected to intertwine with quantum effects. The number marks a limit of the current description, not a clock reading at which an act of creation was measured.

Four layers that are often confused

Layer Scientific status What it does not prove
Hot expanding universe Supported by multiple lines of observational evidence Does not equal creation from nothing
Cosmic inflation A family of models explaining features of early structure; its mechanism is unsettled Does not by itself determine an absolute beginning
Classical singularity A boundary that appears in relativity solutions under certain conditions Not an object or an observed moment
Quantum gravity Competing research programs and models No established model of what came “before”

Inflation is the name for a family of ideas about extremely rapid accelerated expansion in a stage earlier than the well-described hot phase. Some inflationary models can generate the seeds of fluctuations that later grow into galaxies, and some of their broad predictions are consistent with maps of the cosmic microwave background. But that agreement does not identify the field that caused inflation or prove that any one detailed scenario occurred; NASA notes that its physical mechanism remains unknown. It is therefore not correct to treat “the universe began with inflation” as an observational description of the first moment.

The boundary we observe and the boundary we calculate

The question “what came before?” may look like one question, but it hides three different boundaries. The observational boundary is the oldest electromagnetic radiation that reaches us directly: the cosmic microwave background, after the universe cooled enough for light to travel over large distances. The reconstruction boundary reaches earlier; we infer the physics of the first minutes from light-element abundances and still earlier conditions from patterns in the background, using laws tested in other domains. The theory-validity boundary is where extrapolation with our present equations is no longer secure. These boundaries do not coincide at a single clock time.

Imagine a historian who finds a dated letter, an older administrative record, and then infers from the style of writing that a school existed before both documents. The letter is something the historian read; the record is another witness; the school is an interpretation whose evidential strength can vary. Lacking a “first” page does not mean the city’s history began on the date of the letter, and it does not license a certain story about what preceded the archive. Cosmology uses different tools, but the ordering of evidence, inference, and open question remains necessary. Direct observation of the background therefore says nothing directly about whether there was a pre-inflationary phase; that is tested through theoretical proposals and any effects they might leave, if those effects can be distinguished from alternatives.

There is also a precise difference between “the beginning of the hot phase we know” and “the beginning of all possible time.” The first is a subject of detailed physical research; the second requires knowledge of the structure of time at a boundary where classical gravity may fail. The breadth of our ignorance about the second does not make the evidence for the first illusory. Nor does the success of the first model entitle us to claim that the second question has been solved. NASA’s overview of early cosmic history and the scope of the BGV theorem show why this separation matters.

Does a singularity mean time began?

When some solutions of general relativity are run backward, quantities such as density and curvature tend toward infinity and trajectories become inextendible; we call this a singularity. A singularity, however, is not a tiny point whose physics we understand, but a sign that the classical description has lost its power. Just as an equation breaking down at a zero denominator does not by itself tell us what exists in the world, a classical infinity does not by itself tell us what physically happened.

Singularity theorems establish powerful results under geometric and physical conditions. But they do not provide a theory of quantum gravity. Two readings therefore must not be confused: the boundary may reflect a genuine beginning of time, or it may be a doorway to a deeper description that removes the singularity. Deciding which is correct requires a theory and tests, not a choice of wording.

What the BGV theorem proves—and what it does not

Borde, Guth, and Vilenkin showed that a spacetime with positive average expansion along a past-directed path cannot be complete along that path. The result matters because it does not depend on the same traditional energy conditions, and it prevents some versions of eternal inflation from serving as a self-contained explanation extending indefinitely into the past.

But “past-incomplete” does not mean “created from nothing.” It means that the geodesic description being used does not extend without limit and that other physics is required at the boundary. The theorem does not determine the nature of that physics, exclude every bouncing or emergent universe, or turn metaphysical nothingness into a measurable variable. Using it either as a complete empirical proof of creation or as a refutation of faith goes beyond what the theorem says.

The original Borde–Guth–Vilenkin paper formulates the result as the past incompleteness of timelike or null trajectories in sufficiently expanding models and says that inflation needs other physics at one of its boundaries. “Other physics is needed” is not a name for philosophical nothingness, nor a statement that every possible history ends at one point. The incompleteness of a path in a geometric description also does not by itself tell us whether time has a real boundary or whether it is redefined there. The condition of average expansion and the type of path should therefore be stated whenever the theorem is invoked in a theological argument. The original paper’s abstract defines its scope.

What do models propose about “before”?

The no-boundary proposal: In Hartle–Hawking formulations, the early history becomes analogous to a smooth surface with no classical temporal boundary lying behind it. “No boundary” does not mean no explanation; it changes the structure of the question within a quantum model of the universe.

Bounce models: In some applications of loop quantum cosmology, quantum-geometric effects replace the singularity with a bounce connecting a contracting phase to an expanding one. The result is robust within certain simplified models, but it is not yet the established history of the observed universe. Moving from a homogeneous model to a full universe, and extracting distinctive observational signatures, remain active research problems.

Cyclic and emergent models: These propose cycles or a long-lived state preceding hot expansion. They face questions about stability, entropy, and the construction of the perturbation spectrum. The existence of workable equations does not mean that the scenario occurred in nature.

Quantum creation or tunnelling: These are sometimes described with the phrase “a universe from nothing,” but the “nothing” in such a formulation may be a quantum state or the absence of classical spacetime within a mathematical law, not absolute nothingness with no law and no possibility. The term should therefore not be carried from a physics paper into philosophy without definition.

Could the word “before” be meaningless?

“Before” is a temporal relation. If physical time itself has a boundary, asking “what happened before it?” becomes—only as an approximation—like asking what lies north of the North Pole: the sentence is grammatically meaningful, but the coordinate being used has ended. This is a possibility, not an established fact. If time emerges from a deeper structure, there may be a prior ordering that is not “time” in the everyday sense. If there was a bounce, the question gains a temporal meaning within that model.

Methodologically, the answer is neither that the question is foolish nor that any answer is acceptable. Rather, the meaning of the question depends on the theory of time, and the theories are still competing. This is more precise than two opposite popular claims: “we know for certain that there was nothing” and “science knows for certain that there was a previous universe.”

Does physics create a universe from nothing?

Philosophical nothingness is not a quantum vacuum. A quantum vacuum has fields, structure, laws, and possible states. Even a formulation that does not assume classical spacetime still uses a space of possibilities, an action or equation, and rules of calculation. A particle-physics experiment by itself therefore cannot settle the question: why do laws and possibilities exist at all rather than there being nothing?

In the other direction, a “God of the gaps” should not be inserted where a theory is unknown. The failure of a current model does not prove a specific religious mechanism, and discovering a deeper description does not eliminate the question of creation. The creation argument in this article does not depend on a temporal gap in an equation; it asks whether the world and its laws require an explanation for their existence. That is a philosophical and religious argument, not an observational result imposed by the hot Big Bang model. A person may believe in a universe with a beginning, or in created time whose boundary structure we do not know; an equation does not become a creator merely because it describes regularity.

What does the Qur’an say—and what does it not say?

God says: “Have those who disbelieved not considered that the heavens and the earth were a joined entity, and We separated them” [Qur’an 21:30]. The verse presents an image of joining and separation, and exegetes have recorded meanings including the joining of heaven and earth followed by separation, and the sky being closed to rain and the earth to vegetation before both were opened. This historical breadth of interpretation prevents reducing the wording to a single modern cosmological term.

This does not mean that every reported interpretation has equal weight. After presenting views about joining, separation, and the plurality of the heavens, al-Tabari preferred the meaning that the sky was opened with rain and the earth with vegetation; he supported it with the phrase that follows immediately: “And We made from water every living thing.” Another exegete may prefer a different view, but al-Tabari’s preference cannot be omitted and then the modern cosmological reading presented as if it were his interpretation. Al-Tabari’s text places the reader before that detail rather than a slogan of easy “matching.”

A believer may find the image of separation suggestive when contemplating an evolving universe, but it is not correct to say that the verse contains inflation, Planck time, or a determination of cosmic geometry. The Qur’an directs attention to creation and its significance; it does not provide a table of ΛCDM parameters. When it says, “All it takes, when He wills something to be, is simply to say to it: ‘Be!’ And it is!” [Qur’an 36:82], it affirms the sovereignty of divine will, not the erasure of the regularities and stages studied by science.

The Qur’anic question, “Or were they created by nothing, or are they their own creators?” [Qur’an 52:35], confronts human beings with the question of their origin and their capacity to bring themselves into existence. It should not be turned into a statement about a physical state preceding the universe: the address asks its hearers about their creation; it does not describe a quantum vacuum or a cosmological equation. It opens a rational inquiry into the origin of what exists and agency, but it is not a telescope result. Its argumentative force is philosophical and religious; measurement of the cosmic microwave background has the different task of constraining a thermal history and model parameters. The two levels can meet as long as their distinction remains visible.

When the Qur’an calls us to look at beginnings

The connection between the question and the book’s title goes deeper than sharing the word “beginning.” The verse from which the title is taken says: “Travel throughout the land and see how He originated the creation, then Allah will bring it into being one more time” [Qur’an 29:20]. Al-Tabari explains that it addresses those who deny resurrection by arguing for re-creation from the first creation. The verse is not an instruction to measure zero second, nor a claim that travel alone will reveal what lies beyond Planck time. Its subject is the power to originate and re-create, and its method is reflection on creation. When we study the cosmic background, we practice one form of looking at the world, but we do not extract the doctrine of resurrection from its spectrum. Al-Tabari’s commentary on the verse shows the place of the religious argument from which the book begins.

Even if a model with no classical temporal boundary were established in the future, “therefore it needs no Creator” would not automatically follow; describing the extension of time is one question, while claiming that the world and its laws exist independently is another. Conversely, the present model’s breakdown near Planck time is not a special proof that one particular interpretation of the verses is correct. If the book’s argument is to be defended, its strength lies in not hanging it on a temporary gap in physics or a number from a revisable model.

If the physics of cosmic beginnings changes in the future, our picture of what lies beyond the hot phase may change with it. A Qur’anic verse whose context calls for reflection and faith does not change for that reason. Looking at the universe therefore remains open-ended work: we accept what is established about its history, and ask about creation and meaning without attributing to observation what observation did not observe.

Can the James Webb Space Telescope see “before the Big Bang”?

No. Webb observes light from stars and galaxies that formed after the universe cooled enough for atoms and then stars to become possible. It does not directly see the phase before the cosmic microwave background, much less a hypothetical phase before the hot universe. NASA explains Webb’s observational range: the background itself records a universe about 380,000 years after the beginning of hot expansion, while Webb explores later generations of stars and galaxies. Finding a galaxy older than expected may change a model of galaxy formation, but it does not thereby become a direct observation of what was “before” time.

The useful question when a new discovery is reported is not “did this destroy the Big Bang?” but: which specific hypothesis was tested, which era was observed, and does the result require revising the history of galaxy formation or the history of hot expansion itself? The article can then serve as a way to read the next astronomy headline instead of forcing the reader either to believe a sensational title or reject it wholesale.

Where does knowledge stand now?

We know that the observable universe passed through a hot, dense phase and has been expanding for about 13.8 billion years, and we do not know decisively whether that history has a first boundary, a prior phase, or a description in which “before” is not a valid temporal question. The strongest scientific position is to rank degrees of confidence rather than fill the gap with certainty.

This does not reduce creation to a gap. Belief in a Creator is broader than our ignorance about the first microsecond, just as physics is more disciplined than a metaphysical slogan. Once we know what an equation says and what it does not say, the question becomes deeper: not only what came before, but why there is a world with regularities that reason can read.

Frequently asked questions

What happened before the Big Bang?

Science has no definitive empirical answer. Competing models make time bounded, propose an earlier phase or a bounce, and none has been established by observation.

Did the Big Bang create the universe from nothing?

No. The hot Big Bang model describes the evolution of the universe from an early state of extreme heat and density; metaphysical nothingness and creation are not variables inside the model’s equations.

Does the BGV theorem prove that the universe had an absolute beginning?

No. The theorem establishes past incompleteness for classes of universes with positive average expansion, indicating that other physics is needed at the boundary; it does not prove creation from nothing.

Does the ratq-and-fatq verse describe the Big Bang?

A believer may see a broad resonance in the image of joining and separation, but the verse does not provide the model’s equations or settle inflation or the pre-hot-universe question. It should therefore not be treated as a decisive technical identity.
