Fire From Green Trees in Quran 36:80: Meaning, Tafsir, and Photosynthesis

The verse is an argument for resurrection before it is a lesson in chemistry. Science reveals another layer: green plants convert sunlight into matter that stores energy, and combustion later releases some of it. Where does tafsir end and scientific reflection begin?

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished: 27 September 2026Sources reviewed: 27 September 2026Independent exegete or plant science review: not yet completed

The short answer

The verse comes near the end of an argument for resurrection: the One who first created human beings and brings fire from green trees can bring decayed bones back to life. Classical commentators explained the image in terms familiar to their first audiences: green trees becoming firewood, or fire being kindled with branches of the markh and ‘afar trees while still green. Modern science adds that plants convert sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide into organic matter that stores chemical energy, some of which combustion later releases. That is a legitimate connection for reflection; it does not mean that “photosynthesis” is the one exclusive meaning unknown to earlier generations.

Where the idea appears in the book “قل سيروا في الأرض”
This investigation expands on pages 134–137 of the book, in the discussion of “Fire, Energy, and Transformation.” The book traces a path from trees and combustion to heat and work, placing the use of nature within a framework of divine power and human responsibility.

The verse in context: answering the question of bones and resurrection

God says: “He presents an example to Us and forgets his own creation. He says, ‘Who will give life to bones when they have crumbled?’ Say, ‘The One who brought them into being the first time will give them life, and He knows every creation—the One who made fire for you from the green tree, and from it you kindle.’” [Ya-Sin 36:78–80].

The question these verses answer is not “How do chloroplasts work?” It is how a person can dismiss re-creation while seeing the original act of creation and the transformations God brings about in nature. The argument moves from humanity’s creation to a tangible scene that seems to bring opposites together: greenness and water, followed by fire and kindling.

Ignoring the context changes the verse’s function. If we isolate its sentence and turn it into a formula about fuel, the argument for resurrection disappears. If we ignore the physical world described, the invitation to observe is also lost. A balanced reading starts with the Quranic purpose and then asks what science explains about plant matter, combustion, and energy.

What did classical commentators say?

Al-Tabari reports interpretations referring to any tree that can serve as fuel after being green, as well as interpretations specifically naming the markh and ‘afar trees, whose branches Arabs used to strike fire. Al-Baghawi mentions rubbing two branches together to kindle a flame, by God’s permission. This reference to a familiar practice explains why the argument was intelligible to its first hearers before modern chemistry.

Ibn Kathir describes a fresh tree, originally nourished by water, becoming firewood that people burn, and treats that transformation as evidence that the One capable of it can resurrect the dead. Al-Qurtubi discusses the production of burning dry material from a moist branch and also reports the markh and ‘afar interpretations. Ibn ‘Ashur emphasizes the sentence’s place within the argument, as a visible example of something being brought forth from a condition that seems unlike it.

These readings share two foundations: the scene demonstrates divine power, and the audience knows that fire can be kindled from trees. The original argument does not need to wait for the discovery of chlorophyll. It would therefore be wrong to portray early commentators as incapable of understanding the verse and claim that modern science found its meaning for the first time.

Does flame come straight from a green branch?

Green wood contains a high proportion of water, making it generally harder to ignite than dry wood. Water absorbs part of the heat and evaporates before combustion can continue. But “harder to ignite” is not the same as “impossible for green plant material to lead to fire.”

Classical interpretations bring together two different pathways:

  1. Fire-making by friction: suitable plant material is used to create heat and an ember or spark through friction, which can then ignite more easily combustible material. Moisture, hardness, and structure affect success.
  2. Fuel: a tree that was once green dries out, and its wood becomes fuel. The greenness recalls the origin of the once-living material before it turned into firewood.

Not every green leaf needs to be a matchstick, and not every wet branch must ignite merely from rubbing. The verse says that God has provided people with fire from green trees. The commentators explain forms of use known to people, not a law that all green plants produce flames in the same way.

What does photosynthesis do?

During photosynthesis, plants absorb light and use water and carbon dioxide to build sugars and other organic compounds. Oxygen is released during oxygenic photosynthesis. NASA’s overview of the carbon cycle explains that plants absorb carbon dioxide and sunlight to make glucose and other sugars used to build their tissues; bonds in carbon-based structures store energy that can later be released when those structures break down.

A simplified teaching equation is:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

This formula is useful, but compresses a complex network of light reactions, electron transport, carbon fixation, and the synthesis of many different compounds. A plant does not “fill itself with fire”; it converts a small portion of light energy into chemical energy within its organic matter.

Here lies a meaningful connection to the verse: material that looks green and moist participates, during growth, in converting light energy into organic structures that can later be burned as fuel. This is a modern account of the physical and chemical process, not a replacement for the verse’s meaning or proof that the expression “green trees” technically means “photosynthesis.”

How does plant matter turn into heat and light?

Combustion is a relatively rapid oxidation reaction. When wood is heated, its components begin to undergo thermal decomposition and release flammable gases. Those gases and the remaining charcoal react with oxygen, producing substances including carbon dioxide and water vapor while releasing heat and light.

Fire does not create energy out of nothing. Differences in chemical energy between reactants and products appear as heat, motion, and radiation. Nor are wood and oxygen alone sufficient: the process needs an initial heat input high enough for ignition. The “fire triangle” therefore identifies three practical conditions: fuel, an oxidizer, and heat.

The US Department of Energy describes combustion as one means of converting biomass into useful energy, while the US Energy Information Administration explains that biomass is renewable organic material that can be burned directly for heat or processed into fuel. These references explain technology; they do not replace the works of tafsir in interpreting Surat Ya-Sin.

Do greenness and moisture contradict a tree’s ability to burn?

Greenness describes a living plant’s condition, including water and chlorophyll; it does not mean that every part of the plant is water or incapable of becoming fuel. Plant tissue also consists of cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, sugars, and other carbon-based compounds. This matter did not appear from nothing: the plant built much of it using atmospheric carbon and light energy. Water and organic matter can therefore coexist in the same branch without contradiction.

In practice, water consumes heat as it evaporates, which makes damp wood difficult to ignite and its combustion less efficient and smokier. When wood dries, that energy cost falls; heating can then decompose its components and release gases that burn in oxygen. The verse does not require a green leaf to shoot flames when touched. It is enough that green trees provide the original material for kindling, alongside the markh and ‘afar method discussed by commentators.

This distinction also corrects a familiar but imprecise analogy: fire is not “sunlight itself” stored in a trunk like a box. More precisely, light drives reactions that build higher-energy molecules; combustion rearranges atoms into more stable products and releases the energy difference. Energy is transformed, matter participates in a carbon cycle, and scientific language explains one aspect of the natural process without replacing the Quran’s argument.

Is the verse a “scientific miracle” predicting photosynthesis?

The answer depends on what the phrase means. If it means that modern science reveals a deeper aspect of nature made useful to humanity—that plant greenness relates to converting light into chemical energy, which plant matter can later release as heat—then this is reasonable reflection as long as its limits are stated.

But if it means the verse secretly stated chlorophyll, a carbon-fixation equation, or fossil fuels, and that this alone is its meaning, the claim exceeds the text. The verse does not mention sunlight, carbon dioxide, or oxygen, and its original audiences already understood it through the observable phenomenon of kindling.

Does the verse mean oil and coal?

Coal formed mainly from ancient plant remains buried and subjected to pressure and heat over geological periods. Much oil and gas, by contrast, derives from microscopic organic matter, including algae and marine organisms—not simply from “forests that turned directly into oil,” as popular accounts sometimes suggest. Grouping all fossil fuels under the word “tree” and calling that the verse’s original meaning oversimplifies both the science and the interpretation.

It is fair to observe that much chemical energy in organic matter ultimately traces back to sunlight and carbon fixation. But the verse does not need that chain for its argument to stand, and not every source of carbon-based energy can be made a translation of “green tree.”

Where is the deeper precision?

Precision is not found by hunting for a hidden scientific term. It lies in holding together several levels that do not cancel one another:

  • A tree is alive and moist, yet people can kindle fire from it.
  • Plants build their matter through light, water, and air.
  • Organic matter retains chemical energy that can be transformed.
  • Fire brings both benefits and dangers and requires conditions and quantities.
  • The whole image returns, within the surah, to the power of creating and restoring life.

On this reading, science neither opposes nor replaces tafsir. Exegesis establishes context, language, and argument; science explains aspects of how trees and combustion work. Confusing these domains invites exaggeration, while completely separating them misses an opportunity for observation and reflection.

The strongest objection: is this a modern projection?

It is a projection if we assert that the verse exclusively means “photosynthesis” or announced details it never stated. It is not a projection when we start with the familiar meaning in context and commentary, then use botany and chemistry to understand an aspect of the creation illustrated by the phenomenon.

The standard is simple: could the modern term be removed while the verse still makes sense? Yes. Does the modern term genuinely explain how light can become organic matter and later heat? Yes. The relationship is therefore a limited scientific elaboration, not an exact correspondence or a “secret code.”

Frequently asked questions

What does “fire from green trees” mean?

The God who brought fire forth for people from green trees can restore decayed bones to life. Commentators described both wood that later becomes firewood and the markh and ‘afar trees from which Arabs made fire.

Does the verse mean photosynthesis?

It is not an explicit lesson in photosynthesis, and its meaning should not be restricted to that process. Photosynthesis nevertheless explains how plants store light energy in organic matter that can later burn.

How can fire come from a tree containing water?

Moisture delays ignition but does not remove the organic matter that stores energy. Some plant materials can be used to strike fire, and wood that was once green becomes fuel after drying.

Are markh and ‘afar real trees?

Their names appear in Arabic commentary as trees whose branches were used to kindle fire. Evidence of the historical practice does not make every botanical detail in transmitted accounts equally well verified by modern research.

Do oil and coal mean the green tree mentioned in the verse?

There is no evidence that the verse is restricted to those substances. Their organic origins and distant relationship to photosynthesis can be discussed, but that is later reflection, not an exclusive interpretation.

Review status and limitations

This is an author-led review combining the Quranic context, identified commentaries, and official scientific references on the carbon cycle, photosynthesis, and biomass. It has not yet undergone independent review by an exegete or a specialist in plant physiology and combustion. The book inspired the question and the structure of the connection; it is not the source of the scientific facts.
