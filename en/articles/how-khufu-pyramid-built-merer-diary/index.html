How Was Khufu’s Great Pyramid Built? What Merer’s Diary Actually Reveals

A 4,500-year-old work log records stone, boats, days, and officials. It offers an unusually direct view of Khufu’s logistics—but not the lost plan of a ramp or an answer to every construction question.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished: 27 September 2026Sources reviewed: 27 September 2026Independent Egyptologist review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Merer’s Diary does not explain how pyramid blocks were raised to the upper courses, but it documents a crucial part of how the project was carried out. Late in Khufu’s reign, a crew led by Inspector Merer transported white limestone by boat and waterways from the Tura quarries to Akhet-Khufu in Giza. The log records loading, sailing, overnight stays, and arrival dates, and identifies places and officials. It reveals not the “secret of lifting” but a supply chain built on quarries, water, boats, a crew, timekeeping, and administration.

Where this idea appears in the book قل سيروا في الأرض فانظروا كيف بدأ الخلق
This investigation expands pages 83–84 of the book, particularly page 83, where the Wadi al-Jarf papyri provide a window into the logistics of Khufu’s pyramid. The book supplies the original question and civilizational argument; the archaeological facts instead come from the papyri’s publication and excavations at Giza.

What is the Diary of Merer?

“The Diary of Merer” is the name given to two of the best-preserved logs in a papyrus archive discovered in 2013 at the entrances to storage galleries at the Red Sea port of Wadi al-Jarf. The French Institute of Oriental Archaeology (IFAO) dates the archive to late in Khufu’s reign and describes it as the oldest known papyrus archive in Egypt so far.

Merer is not writing his life story or a ceremonial celebration of the pyramid. The document is closer to an operating log: where was the crew on a particular day? Where did it sail from? Where did it spend the night? When did it load stone? Which stop did it reach? That administrative dryness is its strength: the text was not written centuries later to interpret a monument; it was part of the work itself.

The Wadi al-Jarf archive also contains accounts of supplies and materials and records from other crews. The port supported royal expeditions across the Red Sea to southwestern Sinai. This shows that state crews were not confined to one site: men, ships, and materials moved among the port, Nile, quarries, and royal projects.

What do the papyri record about Khufu’s pyramid?

Merer records his crew traveling to the northern and southern Tura quarries on the Nile’s eastern bank, loading limestone onto boats, then sailing toward Giza. Names associated with the project appear:

  • Akhet-Khufu, “Horizon of Khufu,” the name of the pyramid complex.
  • Ro-She Khufu, “Entrance to Khufu’s Basin,” a stop on the water route.
  • Ankhhaf, a senior official named in the log as director of Ro-She Khufu.

The scholarly annex to Pierre Tallet’s publication presents readings and translations of the records and the routes of the journeys. Tallet estimates that Merer’s crew numbered about forty men and could make two or three round trips within a ten-day cycle. This is a scholarly estimate based on the log, not an explicit head count of all pyramid builders.

The publication suggests the documents date to Khufu’s twenty-sixth regnal year, near the end of his reign. The white Tura limestone may therefore have been used for late-stage casing or finishing work. That is a strong possibility, but it is not the same as claiming “every stone in the pyramid came from Tura”: much of the pyramid’s core was quarried on the Giza plateau itself, and other kinds of stone were used in different locations.

How did stone travel from Tura to Giza?

Giza’s geography in Khufu’s time differed from how it looks today. Water channels and basins connected to the Nile extended beside the plateau. The names “Khufu’s Basin” and “Entrance to the Basin” in the papyri fit archaeological evidence for harbors and waterways serving the building area.

Research on canals and harbors in the era of pyramid building at Giza presents evidence about the harbor, basin, and access routes. Boats reduced the cost of moving heavy loads compared with dragging them long distances over land. But reaching the harbor did not mean reaching the pyramid’s top: unloading began another phase that Merer’s log does not describe.

What does the diary not tell us?

The scholarly publication explicitly notes that these texts do not mention the construction techniques used. They therefore do not directly answer:

  • What form the ramp or ramps around the pyramid took.
  • How lifting equipment changed as the building rose.
  • How many people worked on the entire project over the years.
  • The precise cargo of every ship on every voyage.
  • How every stone block was leveled and positioned.

“The papyri revealed the entire method of building the pyramid” is therefore an exaggeration. Conversely, “We have no idea how the Egyptians built it” erases a large body of evidence. We know about quarries, some transportation routes, how crews were organized, the settlement supporting the project, and traces of tools and comparable techniques. Reconstructing the entire lifting system remains debated.

Did slaves build the pyramids?

Films picture crowds of foreign slaves being whipped. Merer’s logs do not support that image: they show Egyptian teams with leaders, subdivisions, records, provisions, and assigned tasks. Excavations of the Lost City of the Pyramid Builders, south of the Sphinx, reveal a large royal settlement with accommodation, workshops, bakeries, storage facilities, and administrative and food-preparation areas.

But rejecting the cinematic image and claiming that everyone held a “freely chosen job” in the modern sense is another leap. An ancient monarchy could impose labor obligations or seasonal service, and the available documents do not record every individual’s legal contract. A more accurate conclusion is: the evidence depicts an organized and logistically supported Egyptian workforce, not merely a story about unknown foreign slaves. The different degrees of obligation and social status among workers require careful distinction rather than a slogan.

What does a small document reveal about a great state?

To the eye, the pyramid is stone; the logs reveal the system that preceded the stone:

  1. Division of labor: a crew, a supervisor, and an administrative hierarchy.
  2. Scheduling: workdays, overnight stops, and travel cycles.
  3. Specialization: quarries, navigation, transport, storage, and administration.
  4. Writing: a record accompanying work rather than reliance on oral memory alone.
  5. Engineering geography: basins, entrances, and waterways serving the project.
  6. Supply: provisions and goods reaching crews moving between distant sites.
  7. Centralization: the state’s capacity to assemble resources and direct them toward one project.

In contemporary terms, the project was not simply “lifting stones” but managing a state-scale supply chain. This directly develops the book’s theme: civilization does not emerge from one resource or some mysterious genius; it emerges when knowledge becomes an institution capable of coordination and repetition.

How can we read a work log without turning it into myth?

The logs matter because they are operational records, not texts written to persuade a modern reader of the pyramid’s greatness. The writer notes what the work required: crew movements, destination, accommodation, receipt of provisions or stone, and the passage of days. Their everyday character leaves less room for embellishment, but it does not eliminate problems of interpretation. Parts of the papyri are missing; some words and places are interpreted through comparison and context; translating an ancient Egyptian title into a modern job description is an approximation, not a perfect equivalence.

We should therefore distinguish three layers. First is the document’s text: surviving names, actions, days, and destinations. Second is archaeological reconstruction: connecting Tura with white limestone, Akhet-Khufu with the pyramid complex, and water routes with harbors and canals found by excavation. Third is engineering inference: what that organization implies about fleet size, delivery speed, and the sequence of construction. The scope for uncertainty increases as we move from the first layer to the third.

This distinction guards against two opposing mistakes: treating every word in the log as a full technical blueprint, and discounting its significance because it does not draw a ramp. The document is strong at what it was written to record: showing that a crew with identified leadership transported a particular material within an administrative apparatus linked to the project. It is weaker when asked to answer questions it never recorded. A good document does not answer everything, but it changes which questions we can ask with confidence and prevents gaps in knowledge from becoming a license for exciting stories unsupported by material or textual evidence.

Does the diary disprove theories about aliens?

The document does not need to mention extraterrestrials to “disprove” them. An extraordinary claim requires positive supporting evidence; it cannot demand that every ancient papyrus explicitly refute it. What the diary offers is methodologically stronger: a specific human route, named people, known quarries, boats, water, schedules, and officials within a connected archaeological context.

An unresolved engineering question is not permission to fill the gap with an unknown cause. Not knowing the final form of the ramp does not undo what we know about transport and administration. Nor does discovering a transport log resolve the lifting engineering on its own. Sound method assigns each piece of evidence its proper place and resists the temptation to “solve the whole mystery,” whether the proposed solution is popular or academic.

The strongest objection: isn’t the papyrus only about casing stone?

Even if the stone transported in the late phase was for the casing, the document is still directly connected to the Akhet-Khufu project. It does not, however, justify claiming that Merer’s crew built the entire pyramid core or transported all its materials. This limitation does not weaken the document; it defines its actual value as a contemporary witness to one documented part of the project, not an encyclopedia of the whole construction.

The article’s strength consequently lies in separating three levels: what the signs on the papyrus say, what an Egyptologist reconstructs from their context, and what remains an engineering hypothesis. Collapsing these levels into a single certainty makes the story more exciting but less truthful. Keeping them separate gives readers knowledge they can scrutinize.

Frequently asked questions

Does Merer’s Diary explain how pyramid stones were raised?

No. It describes the transport of Tura limestone to the pyramid area by water and the organization of voyages. It does not specify the shape of ramps or how blocks were lifted to the upper courses.

What did Merer’s crew carry?

The crew probably transported white Tura limestone for casing late in Khufu’s reign. The document links that material to the Akhet-Khufu complex without recording the ultimate use of every block after delivery.

Do the papyri prove that slaves did not build the pyramids?

They challenge a simplistic picture of anonymous workers with no organization and document Egyptian crews with provisions and leaders. They do not alone determine the legal and social status of every worker.

Who was Merer?

A middle-ranking inspector who led a work and navigation crew. Two papyri preserve records of his team’s work, not a complete personal biography.

Do we now know exactly how the whole pyramid was built?

We know much about materials, quarries, transportation, organization, and some tools, but we have no complete contemporary description of every stage of lifting and assembly. The answer lies between “the entire mystery has been solved” and “we know nothing.”

Review status and limitations

This is an author-led review based on the French Institute of Oriental Archaeology’s publication of the papyri, the results of Giza excavations, and named archaeological sources. It has not yet been independently reviewed by an Egyptologist. The book does not serve as evidence for archaeological facts: it identifies the civilizational question and the passage this investigation develops.
