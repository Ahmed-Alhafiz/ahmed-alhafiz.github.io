Jinn and Demons in the Quran, Gospels and Torah

Three scriptural traditions use distinct concepts. Their narratives do not diagnose a person.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 1 October 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent medical and religious specialist review: not yet completed

Direct answer

The Quran affirms the existence of jinn and describes differences among them. The Gospels tell of unclean spirits and exorcisms. Deuteronomy mentions beings translated as “demons” while condemning worship offered to them. These passages do not use identical concepts, and none is a checklist for diagnosing a living person. This study compares particular texts within their traditions, rather than every belief held by Muslims, Christians and Jews.

This is general educational material by the author, neither an individual medical diagnosis nor a ruling on a person's religious condition. External medical and religious specialist review has not yet been completed. Do not stop prescribed treatment on your own.

Existence and diagnosis are different questions

A search for “are demons real?” may quickly lead to frightening stories and symptom lists. A more useful first step is to ask what each source actually says. In Islam the Quran speaks of jinn; the Gospels narrate encounters with unclean spirits; Deuteronomy condemns worship of other beings. Each has its own literary and religious setting. Bringing them together does not produce a clinical test for an individual.

In Ahmed Alhafiz's forthcoming psychological-horror novel Umm Abbas, Maryam faces competing explanations for her distress. At times the dispute over its cause threatens to eclipse concern for her safety. The novel raises a question about how people use religious language; its invented events are no experimental evidence for possession.

How should the sources be compared?

Keep scripture, commentary and our comparative inference separate. A Quranic verse is not the same as a later exegete's interpretation. Mark is not the later Catholic catechism. Rashi's commentary is not a word of the Torah. A translation selects an approximate word without making two concepts in different languages identical.

“Gospels” here means selected New Testament passages, especially Mark; “Torah” means the Five Books of Moses. This scope does not adjudicate revelation or the history of composition. The narrower question is what each selected passage supports in context.

The Quran: not every jinn is a devil

Quran 72:1 describes a group of jinn listening to the recitation; 72:11 says that some among them are righteous and others are not. In 18:50 Iblis is described as one of the jinn who defied God's command. His disobedience is not a judgment on every member of that category. Quran 6:112 refers to devils among humans and jinn. Ibn Kathir discusses humans who mislead as well, so “devil” in that verse cannot simply stand in for every unseen creature.

For a Muslim, these texts support belief in jinn within the Quran's framework. “Is this particular episode or voice caused by a jinn?” is a separate claim. The existence of a category does not identify the cause of one person's symptoms.

Seeking refuge and fear in Quran 72:6

The verse describes men who sought refuge with men among the jinn and says that rahaq increased. Ibn Kathir discusses the practice of seeking the protection of a supposed lord of a valley and an increase in fear. Al Tabari reports several readings of rahaq, favoring one that concerns the jinn's increased wrongdoing when humans sought their refuge. These exegetical differences should remain visible.

Fear, sin and aggression in historical interpretation are not modern diagnostic labels. In the novel's dark marginal notes on pages 202–203, the verse helps frame how a search for security can become dependence and fear. That is a literary reading, not a verified clinical history of Maryam.

Did Islamic commentary discuss possession?

Yes. In his reading of Quran 2:275, al Qurtubi accepts the possibility of an effect of jinn and possession. It would misrepresent the tradition to claim that all interpreters denied it. Yet the verse's setting concerns usury; the commentary supplies no clinical criteria distinguishing a seizure or psychiatric condition from possession.

The report in Sahih al Bukhari 2038 about Satan circulating like blood arises when the Prophet clarifies to two men that the woman with him is Safiyyah, to prevent suspicion. It should not be turned into an anatomical mechanism of neurological illness. Abu Dawud 3855 includes an instruction to seek treatment; the cited collection displays al Albani's grading. Faith in the unseen does not require abandoning medical care.

Does satanic influence erase human responsibility?

Quran 14:22 speaks of Satan's invitation and his followers' response. Al Saadi explains the denied authority in terms of compelling proof, while distinguishing temptation. The passage concerns invitation, response and accountability in the hereafter. It neither rules out every kind of influence nor describes a neurological pathway. Nor does it make a distressed person morally responsible for an illness.

Mark: narratives of unclean spirits

Mark 5:1–20 narrates an encounter with a man described as having an unclean spirit and his subsequent restoration. Mark 9:14–29 describes a boy's falling, rigidity and foaming, followed in the story by the spirit's departure and the boy's rising. Within a Christian reading, these narratives concern Jesus' authority over unclean spirits. Similarity between an ancient description and a modern symptom neither retroactively diagnoses that character nor identifies the cause of today's patient's symptoms.

A textual detail matters: the US Conference of Catholic Bishops notes that some manuscripts add fasting to prayer in Mark 9:29. “Prayer and fasting” should therefore not be presented as the invariant wording of every witness.

Does Mark make every illness a demon?

Mark 1:29–34 recounts Simon's mother-in-law's fever, other healings and expulsions of demons in distinct descriptions. The narrative itself does not treat every illness as an unclean spirit. It also gives no modern diagnostic rule. The selected Christian passages speak of unclean spirits and demons; calling them “jinn” in every Quranic sense would smuggle in a different tradition's category.

In Mark 5:19–20 the restored man is told to go home to his people, and the text then recounts his proclamation in the Decapolis. The return to human relationships is relevant to a literary reading of Maryam and her family, without equating a fictional character with the figure in Mark.

What does the Catholic catechism say about illness?

Paragraph 1673 recognizes exorcism under particular Church authority and distinguishes it from illness, especially psychological illness, whose treatment belongs to medical science. This is a statement of the cited Catholic teaching, not an automatic description of every Christian denomination. A religious procedure is not itself a validated medical test of what caused symptoms.

Deuteronomy 32:17 and worship

Deuteronomy 32:15–18 concerns turning from God to strange worship. In Chabad's presentation, 32:17 contains the Hebrew shedim, rendered “demons”; its translation and Rashi's comment describe them as lacking power. The USCCB version of the same verse uses “no-gods.” These English formulations differ; comparing their published translations is not an independent philological decision about Hebrew.

The clear point within this passage is condemnation of worship offered to those beings. It neither describes the mechanism of an illness nor establishes that shedim equal Quranic jinn in all respects. Rashi is commenting on the Torah, not speaking as its original text. The passage does not summarize all Jewish approaches to the unseen.

What does this add to Umm Abbas?

On pages 194 and 200, Sheikh Ahmed in the story leaves room for the doctor and resists allowing the family's dispute to swallow Maryam's needs. His conduct contrasts with al Sourani's use of explanation as authority and coercion. The scene's literary force lies in asking whether someone claiming to help protects a person's dignity, acknowledges uncertainty and keeps treatment available. It does not prove that every explanation in the story is true.

The notes on pages 202–203 evoke Quran 72:6 and the relationship between fear, misplaced refuge and the family's fracture. Earlier pages 184–185 also caution that a scene does not establish possession. Belief in jinn within Islam does not entail accepting every allegation about an individual.

Family division in a prophetic report

The note on page 203 invokes Sahih Muslim 2813b, which describes Iblis rewarding the one who separated a man and his wife. It concerns discord between spouses; it should not be stretched into an explanation of every family disagreement or mental illness. Its literary role in the novel is narrower: fear changes how Maryam's parents relate to each other and to her. The report's couple and the novel's whole family network are not the same thing.

In the narrative, Sheikh Ahmed insists on continuing medical care and rejects isolation, unwanted touching and selling fear. These are observations about a fictional character's ethics, not certification of an actual healer or proof that a ritual works.

Can unusual experiences establish jinn?

The US National Institute of Mental Health describes psychosis as a set of symptoms that can include hearing voices, with varied possible causes and a need for professional assessment. Its educational publication does not test for jinn or diagnose a fictional character. Three questions should remain separate: did someone have an experience, what evidence supports a medical explanation, and what does the religious text under discussion mean? A truthful account of hearing something cannot by itself identify the sound's source.

A similar outward description does not guarantee the same cause. Nor does an event that is presently unexplained prove one proposed explanation. “Voice” also does not make intrusive thought, hallucination and dissociation interchangeable; each requires its own context and evaluation. A person's voluntary religious support must never justify coercion or stopping prescribed care. Seek a clinician for persistent or worsening symptoms and local emergency help for an immediate safety risk.

Frequently asked questions

Do all three scriptures describe “jinn” in the same sense?

No. The selected texts allow comparison, but their words, contexts and doctrinal traditions differ.

Do convulsions or hearing voices establish possession?

No. Resemblance to a religious narrative cannot settle the cause of an individual's symptoms. This article interprets texts and does not diagnose people.

Does belief in jinn require stopping medical care?

No. The cited Islamic report encourages treatment; a general belief cannot replace individual assessment.

How does this relate to the novel?

Umm Abbas examines fear, family conflict and claimed healing. Its scenes are literary material, not documented medical cases.

Evidence transparency

Religious texts establish claims within their respective faiths, commentary interprets them, and NIMH provides clinical education. None of these sources validates an individual diagnosis from a fictional scene.
