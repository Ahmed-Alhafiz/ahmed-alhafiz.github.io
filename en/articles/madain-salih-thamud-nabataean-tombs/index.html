Is Madain Salih the Land of Thamud?

Islamic texts link al-Hijr to Thamud; inscriptions and archaeology identify the famous standing tombs as Nabataean.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 28 September 2026English edition: 13 October 2026Independent archaeological and exegetical review: not yet completed

Direct answer

Al-Hijr is associated with Thamud in the Quran and Islamic tradition. This does not mean every carved facade or standing tomb at Madain Salih was made by the people of Thamud. Archaeology and inscriptions identify the best-known monumental tombs, including Qasr al-Farid and Qasr al-Bint, as Nabataean; most belong to a period spanning the first century BCE and first century CE. The place has a long, layered history. Its religious association with Thamud does not assign every surviving structure to that people.

Why does the confusion arise?

Pictures of Madain Salih often appear in books, travel pages and videos with captions such as “houses of Thamud” or “palaces of Thamud.” Repetition can turn a label into an apparently settled fact: this facade was cut by Thamud, and that tomb was one of their houses.

A photograph is simpler than the site's history. Different groups occupied or used the area across time. Moreover, the Quranic reference to houses carved in mountains cannot identify the function of every visible rock-cut building. Many of the most famous facades are Nabataean burial chambers with inscriptions recording owners and rules of use.

Three questions must therefore be kept apart:

  1. Do Islamic texts link al-Hijr to Thamud?
  2. Was the region inhabited before the Nabataeans?
  3. Who carved the famous facades and tombs that survive today?

Confusing those questions produces the mistaken attribution.

What do the Quran and hadith say about al-Hijr?

Quran 15:80–84 refers to the people of al-Hijr, who carved houses out of mountains in a sense of security and were then seized by a cry after denying the messengers. Other passages name Thamud and the prophet Salih. Quran 26:149 also describes carving houses skillfully from mountains.

In a report from Abdullah ibn Umar, the Prophet passed al-Hijr on the way to Tabuk and warned his companions about entering the places of a people who had been punished except with humility and reflection; the report appears in al-Bukhari and Muslim.

There is thus an explicit religious connection between al-Hijr and that story. Yet scripture and hadith offer no archaeological catalogue assigning every facade in the present site to the same people or dating each tomb and inscription. Recognizing that limit does not diminish the texts. It avoids attributing to them a detailed material claim they did not make.

What do today's remains show?

UNESCO describes Hegra, or al-Hijr/Madain Salih, as the largest conserved site of Nabataean civilization south of Petra. It dates the monumental decorated tomb facades broadly between the first century BCE and the first century CE. UNESCO counts 111 monumental tombs, including 94 with decorated facades.

The Royal Commission for AlUla presents Hegra as the principal southern city of the Nabataean kingdom. More than a hundred Nabataean rock-cut tombs remain. The identification does not rest on a superficial visual resemblance: Nabataean inscriptions on entrances name owners and regulate burial rights, ownership and penalties. Letter forms, architectural style and ornament also connect the site with the Nabataean world extending north to Petra.

Consequently, many of the facades most often photographed at Madain Salih are Nabataean tombs. Archaeology has not established them as domestic houses carved by the Quranic Thamud.

Did Thamud build Qasr al-Farid?

Qasr al-Farid, the “lonely castle,” is among Hegra's most recognizable monuments. Its popular name may suggest a residential palace, but it is an unfinished Nabataean tomb cut into a large isolated rock. The unfinished work helps show how masons proceeded from the top of a facade downwards.

There is no credible archaeological evidence identifying it as Salih's house, the house of a Thamudite leader or the place connected with the she-camel. Such labels are popular stories rather than findings from inscription and excavation. The monument's scale and beauty are real; the lesson drawn from it does not require assigning it to an unsupported owner.

Does this mean Thamud never lived in the region?

No. Denying a Thamudite attribution for Nabataean tombs is different from denying the association of al-Hijr with Thamud. The region predates its Nabataean phase, and UNESCO notes earlier inscriptions and rock art at the site. An older trace, however, does not automatically reveal the name of its makers or prove that a particular structure belongs to the Thamud of the Quran.

Archaeologists can investigate occupation layers, date inscriptions, analyze pottery and compare architecture. They cannot assign an unknown object to a historical or religious name without evidence for that assignment.

Conversely, failure to find a structure inscribed “this is the house of Thamud” cannot by itself refute a religious account. Survival depends on reuse, erosion, time and the limits of what has been discovered. The proper result is a bounded claim about present evidence.

Are “Thamudic” inscriptions direct proof?

This term is especially easy to misunderstand. Nineteenth-century scholars used “Thamudic” for large groups of inscriptions in ancient North Arabian alphabets that had not been classified precisely. The Oxford/OCIANA corpus explains that the conventional label does not imply all their writers belonged to the ancient tribe of Thamud, nor that the texts belong to a single period or region.

Finding an inscription called “Thamudic” therefore does not identify its author as one of Salih's people. It is a historical scholarly label for a group of scripts, not an ethnic identity card. That distinction prevents both using inscriptions to prove details they do not carry and alleging that the complexity of terminology somehow contradicts the Quran.

One place can preserve several peoples' histories

An archaeological site is not a frozen picture of one moment. An oasis can be occupied for centuries as trade routes change and new residents arrive. Tombs may be cut near older remains; caravans, pilgrims and railways later pass through the same terrain.

Al-Hijr is such a layered record. Nabataean tombs do not erase what came before; the religious memory of Thamud does not erase what the Nabataeans made after. The error begins when all these layers are compressed into one name and one date.

This is close to the method of Qul Siru fi al-Ard. Traveling through the earth is an invitation to read a trace honestly, separating observation, interpretation and inference rather than seeking a picture that merely confirms a conclusion chosen in advance.

How do archaeologists date the tombs as Nabataean?

The attribution does not depend on an aesthetic impression or an assumption that similar carvings must belong to one people. Several clues converge: inscriptions on some facades, owners' names and formulas concerning burial and property, the shape of Nabataean letters, capitals and facade types, artifacts and building context, and comparison with dated structures elsewhere in the Nabataean world.

Some inscriptions specify who may be buried, who may not open or sell a tomb, and consequences for violation. Those are funerary and legal functions. “Qasr,” or palace, is useful as a modern place name, but it does not determine what the monument was used for or who built it.

Earlier remains demand the same care. A pre-Nabataean petroglyph demonstrates earlier human activity but does not supply a community name automatically. Language, writing style and find context can narrow possibilities, yet attribution remains open when a secure date or identifying evidence is absent. This is a candid description of what the surviving material can tell us.

Does the moral lesson change if the facades are Nabataean?

The Quranic lesson does not depend on the famous photographed facade being the exact building seen by Salih's people. The verses describe a people who carved mountain houses and whose skill did not shield them from consequence. A present visit to a site used by successive peoples invites another reflection: later builders leave remains that will themselves become layers for still later visitors to read.

The problem arises when an admonition is made dependent on an attribution without evidence. A reader who discovers that Qasr al-Farid is Nabataean might then wrongly think that the religious connection of al-Hijr to Thamud has been refuted. Keeping separate the text, the identification of a place and the dating of each structure preserves both the lesson and archaeological accuracy.

Walking through the land thus becomes an intellectual and ethical exercise: examine an object, ask who made it, when and for what purpose, state how sure we are, and then reflect. We should not begin with a desired conclusion and force the stone to endorse it.

Final answer

Al-Hijr is the place associated with Thamud in Islamic texts, but the prominent surviving monuments at Madain Salih do not all belong to Thamud. Most famous tombs and facades are Nabataean and later, while the site also preserves traces of earlier and varied activity. The identity of a place in religious memory and the history of each surviving structure are distinct questions.

That distinction protects the Quranic lesson from an inaccurate archaeological attribution. Looking at the earth should mean reading what remains with care and reflecting on strength, loss and human responsibility.

Frequently asked questions

Is Madain Salih the al-Hijr of the Quran?

Islamic tradition associates al-Hijr with Thamud, and the archaeological site called Hegra or Madain Salih is in that region. Identifying the place does not assign every surviving structure to the same period or people.

Is Qasr al-Farid one of the houses of Thamud?

No direct archaeological evidence establishes that. It is an unfinished Nabataean tomb, consistent with the tomb architecture of Hegra.

Why are some inscriptions called “Thamudic”?

The term is an older classification for groups of ancient North Arabian inscriptions. It does not prove every writer belonged to the Thamud named in the Quran.

Does archaeology disprove Thamud's presence in the area?

No. Dating the visible tombs as Nabataean neither erases an older history nor refutes the religious association. It prevents assigning each extant monument to Thamud without direct evidence.
