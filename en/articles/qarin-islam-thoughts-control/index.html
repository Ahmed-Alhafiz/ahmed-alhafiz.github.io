Qareen in Islam: Can It Read Your Mind or Control You?

What is the qareen in Islam? A reading of hadith, the Qur'an and scholarly views on its knowledge of our inner life, and the limits of linking it to thoughts and decisions, beyond unverified stories.

By Ahmed Alhafiz — أحمد الحافظEnglish edition: 28 October 2026Arabic original: 27 October 2026 · Arabic source review: 27 October 2026 · Independent specialist review: not yet completed

The thought may be fleeting, but the question that follows stays: did someone hear it? Then you read about the qareen and feel that what happened in your silence has been exposed, and that every thought passing through your mind needs to be guarded.

The stories begin with an authentic hadith, then grow: a qareen who knows all your secrets, takes your appearance, chooses your dreams and obstructs your decisions. As these attributes accumulate, it becomes difficult to distinguish what the text says from what the story has added.

Sahih Muslim reports that a person has a companion from among the jinn. But the hadith does not establish every power attributed to this companion in popular videos, or make every troubling thought evidence of its intervention. The question of its knowledge of our inner life requires an accurate account of scholarly views, distinguishing these from knowledge of the unseen or absolute control over a person. [1]

What Is the Qareen in Islam?

'Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud, may God be pleased with him, narrated that the Messenger of God, may God bless him and grant him peace, said: "There is none of you but a companion from among the jinn has been assigned to him." They asked, "Even you, Messenger of God?" He replied, "Even me, except that God has helped me against him, so he became Muslim [fa-aslama], and he only tells me to do good." Muslim recorded it as hadith 2814a. [1]

Regarding the word fa-aslam, al-Nawawi explains two well-known readings. With the final mim vowelled u, it means that the Prophet, may God bless him and grant him peace, is safe from the companion's evil and temptation. With it vowelled a, it means that the companion became Muslim; the meaning of surrendering and submitting is also mentioned. Al-Nawawi prefers the latter reading while reporting the scholars' disagreement over which to favour. Presenting both preserves the narration's meaning without making what was said about the Prophet a description of every person's relationship with their qareen. [1]

The hadith affirms the existence of the companion from among the jinn and mentions the help God granted His Prophet against him. The image of a "hidden double" who repeats a person's features and lives an entire life in their name is not a description found in this hadith. Anyone attributing an additional power to the qareen needs evidence for it, rather than merely repeating the name alongside it.

The Qur'an says: "Whoever turns away from the remembrance of the Most Merciful, We appoint for him a devil, who becomes his companion" [Az-Zukhruf 43:36]. Al-Tabari explains this in the context of turning away and being led astray. The passage concerns companionship and being turned from guidance; it cannot properly be made into a diagnosis for every sadness or disturbance a person experiences. [2]

Is Every Qareen Mentioned in the Qur'an a Devil?

The answer requires reading the context. In Surah Qaf: "His companion will say, 'Here is what I have, ready'" [Qaf 50:23]. Al-Qurtubi reports that a number of commentators identify the companion here as the angel assigned to the person, and mentions another interpretation. A few verses later: "His companion will say, 'Our Lord, I did not make him transgress; rather, he was far astray'" [Qaf 50:27]. Al-Tabari explains this companion as the devil assigned to him in worldly life. [3]

Context determines what is meant. If we gather every occurrence of the word qareen and attach all its attributes to a single being, we may make the text say what it did not. Companionship is a meaning shared by these uses, while the companion's identity still requires reading each passage.

Does the Qareen Know Your Thoughts and Secrets?

Two questions should be kept separate: can it become aware of something a person intends? And does it know the unseen and everything concealed from other people? Combining the questions in a single answer creates confusion.

On what is within the heart, Islamweb quotes Shaykh Ibn Baz as saying that the devil is aware, by God's decree, of what a person wants and intends of good and evil. The site describes the issue as a matter of scholarly discussion. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that denying any awareness is a matter of agreement, or to turn this view into proof of comprehensive knowledge of every detail of a person's life. [4]

As for claims to knowledge of the unseen, in the account of Solomon's death, peace be upon him, God says: "When he fell, it became clear to the jinn that, had they known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating punishment" [Saba' 34:14]. Ibn Kathir explains that his death remained hidden from the jinn subjected to his service. This text places limits on the knowledge claimed for them; a story told by someone claiming to hold the secret of the future cannot override it. [5]

Another step is often forgotten. Even if a view concerning the qareen's abilities is reported, how has it been established that someone speaking to you gets their information from it? Knowing a relative's name or an old incident establishes that they knew that information; how they acquired it still requires verification. A religious possibility does not exempt an individual's claim from the need for evidence.

Can the Qareen Control Your Decisions?

In the Qur'anic account of Satan's words on the Day of Resurrection, "I had no authority over you except that I called you, and you responded to me" [Ibrahim 14:22], a distinction appears between calling and responding. Al-Tabari explains authority here as an argument establishing the truth of his words. The word qareen alone therefore cannot establish that a person is a puppet whose choices have no meaning. [6]

Conversely, we cannot derive from this verse a medical judgment about someone who has lost control of their behaviour or cannot remember what they did. Difficulties involving memory, awareness and behaviour require understanding their circumstances and assessing them. An accusation of sin or a ready-made explanation involving the unseen does not settle them.

If your concern is an action you do not remember, read the article on memory gaps and actions we cannot recall. Describing what happened, when it happened and its circumstances gives anyone helping you clearer information than the name "qareen" alone.

Why Does a Thought Return When I Try to Push It Away?

Fear of the qareen may begin with trying to check that your mind is silent. You monitor what you are thinking, then try not to have a particular thought, and it returns. At that moment, its return may seem like an answer from someone else who knows what you are resisting.

But difficulty pushing a thought away is not, on its own, evidence of a source outside the mind. In an experiment described by Daniel Wegner and colleagues in 1987, participants were asked to describe the flow of their thoughts while trying not to think of a white bear, and to ring a bell whenever they did. In a later phase after suppression, they thought about the bear more than participants who had been asked to think about it from the outset. [7]

The experiment suggested that attempts to suppress a thought may backfire in some circumstances. It matters here because it tests a way of dealing with thoughts, not because it tests or disproves the existence of jinn. Nor does it mean that every attempt to regulate attention will fail, or diagnose the cause of a particular reader's thoughts.

When a thought returns, its return therefore need not become an experiment to prove the qareen: "I told myself not to think, the thought returned, so he is answering me." The first part describes what happened; the conclusion adds something that requires further evidence.

Does a Disturbing Thought Reveal What I Really Want?

In Sahih Muslim, Abu Hurayrah, may God be pleased with him, narrates the Prophet's words, may God bless him and grant him peace: "God has overlooked for my community what they say within themselves, as long as they do not speak of it or act upon it." This is hadith 127a, authentic and included by Muslim in his Sahih. [8]

This deserves care. A passing thought is not equivalent to an action its owner has chosen, and a person cannot properly judge their faith merely because a thought they disliked came to them. The details of resolve, words and actions have their own discussion; a general statement about thoughts does not sum them up.

If recurring thoughts exhaust you, their effect on your life deserves to be understood, even when you do not know why they began. The article on unwanted intrusive thoughts discusses this question in greater detail.

Maryam and the Fear of Being Known from Within

At the beginning of my novel Umm Abbas: Bringing the Beloved and Reuniting a Divorced Woman with Her Husband, Maryam hears her mother calling and quickly closes her phone. The narration describes her capacity to feel guilty before doing anything wrong. This moment needs no supernatural event to reveal something of her world: a question coming from beyond the door is enough to set an internal trial in motion. [9]

Her mother's call is brief, but Maryam hears behind it a question not yet asked. Closing the phone becomes quicker than answering. The scene reveals fear's capacity to precede knowledge: the young woman feels exposed before knowing what her mother saw or wanted to ask.

This literary moment approaches the anxiety of a reader asking, "Does he know what I am thinking?" The feeling of exposure deserves to be understood, but does not, on its own, establish that anyone has accessed a person's inner life. Maryam is a fictional character whose fear we are reading; her scene does not provide information about the qareen's powers.

How Can I Deal with Fear of the Qareen?

Begin by identifying what concerns you: is it a religious question, a recurring thought, an experience during sleep, or an action you cannot remember? Each question has a more appropriate route than gathering them all under a single name.

For a religious question, ask for the text and an explanation with a known source, and distinguish what is established from a view about its interpretation. For an experience affecting your sleep, memory or life, seek a qualified assessment and explain what is happening as well as you can describe it. You do not need to settle questions of the unseen to deserve help.

Nor should "your qareen knows everything" be a reason to surrender your privacy to whoever says it. Ask what establishes their claim, and pause at promises that make safety depend on a secret only they possess, or on payments renewed whenever fear returns.

A thought may pass through your mind while you are getting ready to go out, talking to someone you love, or trying to sleep. Its arrival does not require you to stop your day and put it on trial: who sent it, who heard it, and what does it reveal about you? You can return to what you were doing, and seek help if returning becomes difficult.

The religious question retains its answer, to be sought from its source, and suffering retains its claim to care. What you cannot explain now does not give someone else the right to claim complete knowledge of you. You may ask, verify, and let a passing thought go without surrendering the rest of your day to it.
