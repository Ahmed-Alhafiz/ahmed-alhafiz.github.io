Why Writing Changed Human History

Written records let knowledge, accounts and rights cross generations. Their value depends on readers, care and justice.

By Ahmed AlhafizPublished in Arabic: 28 September 2026English edition updated: 15 October 2026Independent historical and exegetical review: not yet completed

If the record vanished

A clay tablet in the Metropolitan Museum’s collection records an account of grain distribution. We do not know what the worker said on receiving a share, but we see the trace of an accounting operation that survived both writer and worker. Before we read the tablet, the city’s walls tell us nothing about the amount of barley recorded. The preserved tablet is small, but its significance exceeds its size.

Writing changed human history by moving part of memory from bodies to a lasting medium. An account, contract, law or observation could be recorded, copied, transported and compared with another account. A surviving record may be false or mistaken, and oral societies developed knowledge and civilization before these tablets. The change was that separated groups and generations who never met could work with relatively stable material, learn from it, dispute it and correct it.

The idea’s place in Qul Siru fi al-Ard
Pages 43–47 of the book trace a path from names and expression to the pen, and from individual knowledge to a civilization’s memory. Those pages give rise to our question: what did Mesopotamian tablets and the Rhind Papyrus preserve, and what does the image of ink in the Tigris hide when taken literally?

What did writing add to memory?

Human memory is powerful in recognition and storytelling, but it is limited and can change during recall. A written mark fixes a form that can be consulted after the speech is over. An absent person can read it; a later generation can compare it with another copy. Living memory does not disappear. A system of minds, readers, media and archives develops alongside it.

Imagine a clerk reviewing a barley amount on a tablet after a harvest. Numbers do not grow the field or reveal whether the official is honest, but they allow comparison of entries with stock and today’s share with yesterday’s. If two copies disagree, that disagreement itself can be examined. This is an illustration: the tablet before us does not contain a story of seasonal stock audits. Research on cognitive artifacts notes that an external medium can change what people can calculate and coordinate, while warning that the surviving object alone cannot reconstruct every ancient mental practice. Hohol and colleagues’ review states this limit clearly.

This “external memory” does more than store: it reshapes mental work. When we write a problem, we can see its steps, return to an earlier line and change a hypothesis without holding every detail in mind. Multiple records can bring together a long sequence of observations that no single person could experience. Accumulation therefore depends less on a teacher’s lifespan and a pupil’s proximity.

Why do early texts often concern accounts and administration?

Writing developed in more than one region; a single universal origin should not be asserted without evidence. In southern Mesopotamia, marks from the late fourth millennium BCE are closely connected with institutions, commodities and labor. The Metropolitan Museum displays tablets recording grain and other goods, and traces how cuneiform later came to represent language, poetry, law and knowledge beyond accounts.

One tablet should not be made the definitive birth certificate of all human writing. The Metropolitan Museum explains the connection between Uruk tablets, counting goods and institutions, while noting that the system’s origin and spread are more complex than one invention at one moment. The distinction between the oldest surviving material and what first happened matters: archives survive unevenly, and what was lost does not appear in a museum cabinet. Reading the tablet begins with what is written on it, then asks what world of speech, training and practice remained outside it.

The administrative setting has two sides. Writing helps coordinate stores, distribution and calculation; it also gives institutions stronger means to levy, classify and monitor. A record is not automatically good. Its ethical effect depends on who writes, what is counted and who can challenge an entry.

The Rhind Papyrus: a method that can travel

The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus at the British Museum dates to about 1550 BCE and is associated with the scribe Ahmose. It contains dozens of problems and tables involving fractions, multiplication, division, areas and volumes. The museum describes it as likely a teaching text for scribes, with worked examples of problem solving.

Its importance is not that one sheet “invented mathematics” for Egypt. A procedure became available for study, checking and instruction. A learner can follow steps, a teacher repeat an example, and an institution keep a method beyond its author's lifetime. Writing is a surface for working with an idea, not simply a neutral container after thinking has ended.

How can experience become cumulative knowledge?

Noticing a phenomenon is only a start. To enter a body of knowledge open to criticism or repetition, a result needs a description of conditions, measures, method and outcome. Much skill is transmitted through observation and speech, yet texts, tables and drawings can improve the precision of transfer and make a claim somewhat less dependent on its speaker's authority.

Different artifacts illustrate the point: an account can preserve a quantity and its counterpart, the Rhind Papyrus can teach a computational method, and copies of a text can reveal differences between readings. Survival alone makes none of them trustworthy. A number needs a unit and context; a problem needs a teacher who understands its steps; a manuscript needs scrutiny of provenance and transmission. The record gives a mind a surface on which to work, leaving a community responsible for correcting what was written.

Cumulative culture is older and broader than writing. People taught, copied and innovated orally for very long periods. Writing is better called a powerful multiplier than the sole origin of accumulation. It works with reading communities, teaching, copying, trust, criticism and institutions able to preserve material.

Expression and the pen: why neither replaces the other

The Quran says: “He created the human being; He taught them expression” [Al-Rahman: 3–4], and at the opening of Al-Alaq: “Read, and your Lord is the Most Generous, who taught by the pen, taught the human being what they did not know” [Al-Alaq: 3–5]. Expression makes meaning evident to another; the pen extends meaning’s presence after the speaker is gone. Speech provides tone, dialogue and live correction; writing provides stability and reach. Civilization needs both.

The verses do not imply that humans bring facts into existence merely by naming them. People learn distinctions and relationships, investigate regularities, then become responsible for the use of knowledge. The pen can preserve truth or falsify it, establish a right or sustain an unjust record.

The whole history of writing should not be forced into “who taught by the pen” as though it dates the first clay tablet. Al-Qurtubi notes the value and benefits of learning writing, while the sura moves from “Read” to “taught the human being what they did not know.” The verse affirms the blessing of teaching; it gives neither the year the pen was invented nor the first writer’s identity. Al-Qurtubi’s commentary on Al-Alaq: 4 helps us understand the pen within religious discourse, while tablets and archaeological layers establish the history of human media. Losing this distinction turns a Quranic blessing into an archaeological hypothesis the verse never stated.

Writing and justice: the verse of debt

In the verse of debt: “When you contract a debt for a specified term, write it down” [Al-Baqarah: 282]. The context does not glorify handwriting for its own sake: it protects rights, specifies the term, selects a just scribe, calls for witnesses and prevents harm. A document becomes a structure of trust between parties whose memories or power may differ.

The same verse contains three practical safeguards; writing is not isolated: a scribe “with justice,” dictation by the debtor without diminishing what is owed, and witnesses, with a prohibition on harming either scribe or witness. Even the exception for immediate trade shows that the aim is to preserve rights and reduce doubt, not to turn every human action into paperwork. Ibn Kathir explains that recording deferred debt better preserves its amount and due date and makes testimony more precise; Al-Tabari explains the command to document in context. Ibn Kathir’s commentary and Al-Tabari’s commentary place the technique within a relationship of fairness between two parties, one of whom may be weaker than the other.

The connection is central. Writing extends power; justice directs it. A dishonest record can give falsehood the appearance of order. The verse ties a technique to the character of its writer, the person dictating and those witnessing. A document is not necessarily true, but it can enable accountability when institutions hear both sides and preserve the original.

Baghdad: knowledge as a network, not one building

Abbasid knowledge flourished through a network of patrons, translators, physicians, astronomers, booksellers, libraries and gatherings. The “House of Wisdom” is associated with Baghdad, but its precise role in translation is debated; it should not be pictured as a modern university containing every scholar of the age. Dimitri Gutas studies translation within broader relations of patronage, politics and society. Arabic became a shared medium across religions and regions; bookselling, copying and gatherings were links in knowledge’s circulation.

A book can survive while its readers, teachers or language of use are lost; the paper alone does not recreate all the knowledge once around it. A library can be destroyed while copies held by students or scribes in other cities remain. The real store of knowledge is a network of people, texts and education, rather than one shelf.

Did the Tigris turn black with ink in 1258?

Hulagu's army took Baghdad in 1258. The Abbasid caliphate there ended amid killing and destruction. Later accounts picture books cast into the Tigris until its water turned black with ink or people crossed on volumes. We do not have an independent measurement of the river's color or the number of books. The image belongs to the catastrophe's narrative memory and should not be presented as quantitative evidence.

A correction more important than the water’s color is that disaster in one city did not end science across the Islamic world. Michal Biran studies accounts of the invasion and Baghdad’s rebuilding, warning against depicting it as the end of all Islamic civilization. The fourteenth century itself saw extensive Arabic encyclopedic writing. This does not erase killing, loss or destruction; it prevents inventing an “end of science” to prove writing’s value.

What was broken, and what survived elsewhere? Copies, teachers and gatherings could be lost locally, while a student or copyist carried parts of a tradition to another place. Civilizational memory is a network vulnerable to injury and repair, not a single cupboard whose destruction makes all knowledge disappear. This is historically stronger than a river-color story because it invites investigation of people, texts and institutions after 1258 as well as before.

The other side: writing as an instrument of power

Whoever controls a register can shape definitions of class, ownership, debt and borders. States have used writing for taxation, conscription and law; scribes may monopolize literacy, widening the divide between those who record and those recorded. A mistaken text can last for centuries. Persistence is no certificate of truth.

An archive can exclude experiences that do not fit its categories or preserve a victor's account as the only one. Historians therefore read inscriptions and registers beside material remains, environmental traces and oral accounts, asking whose voice is absent. Criticism of a document is not denial of its value; it is attention to its production, purpose and audience.

Are oral societies less intelligent or civilized?

No. Oral communities developed intricate kinship, law, healing, navigation, poetry and collective memory. Rhythm, recitation and repetition can preserve long compositions with practical accuracy. The distinction concerns structures of storage and transmission, each with benefits and risks, not more or less human intelligence.

Written culture itself depends on spoken explanation. A child does not learn to read from a book alone, and laboratory craft is not conveyed by a protocol alone. Tacit knowledge, such as hand movements, timing and judgment in a particular situation, requires practice with others. The pen expands memory; it does not replace teachers and communities.

Who can read a record, and who remains outside it?

“A civilization’s memory” does not preserve everyone’s life equally. An ancient archive keeps what an institution considered worth recording; we therefore inherit rations, stores and orders more readily than the voices of the worker carrying grain, the woman running a household or the craftsperson teaching a child. A name on a tablet is precious evidence of a person or commodity’s presence; its absence does not prove that a person never lived or worked. The document should be read with its find location and function, and with what material remains, the environment and other texts tell us.

A record is also less useful to someone unable to read or contest it. If only the powerful writer keeps the sole copy, documentation may reinforce an unequal relationship. That is why the Quranic debt verse binds a scribe to justice and prohibits diminishing a right or harming a witness. Paper cannot guarantee fairness. It offers a chance when rules preserve copies and allow both sides to know and challenge what has been written.

Writing is thus a human discovery with a double effect: it extends cooperation across absence and time, and extends authority’s ability to classify and monopolize. What determines its direction is not the shape of the cuneiform mark or our present letters, but who controls education, archives and adjudication. In the verse of debt, a document’s existence is insufficient: the scribe must write justly, the debtor dictates, and the witness must not be harmed. These human conditions prevent the record’s survival from becoming the survival of the strongest party’s account alone.

From clay tablets to digital memory

Printing multiplied copies and lowered their cost. Digital networks made publication nearly immediate and searching millions of pages possible. Quantity brings a new problem: scarcity is not knowledge's only enemy; noise, fabrication and lost context also matter. When copying is easy, provenance, date, edition and chain of custody become more valuable.

Digital records are not inherently immortal. Links fail, platforms close and files change without a log. A civilization's memory requires open formats, multiple copies, descriptions, indexes and institutions maintaining them. “We wrote it” does not mean “we preserved it,” and “we published it” does not mean “we taught it.”

From the pen to memory that can be held accountable

In the book's sequence, the pen is more than a symbol of learning. It supports a record that can be examined: external memory, transmission between generations, documentation of rights and the possibility of criticism. Names distinguish concepts, expression conveys them to someone present, and the pen lets them be encountered after absence. Each stage bears responsibility for truth and justice.

Pages 43–47 lead into the book's account of civilizations. A traveler may see a city's walls, while a tablet can tell of grain allocations, labor obligations or a method for solving a problem. Neither the archive nor the stones suffice alone. A state record may speak as though it represents everyone. Combining it with its find context, buildings and economy makes travel through the earth an inquiry into how people distributed knowledge and power. Without an archive, tools, houses, bones and preserved plants remain evidence; their makers do not vanish from history merely because they did not write.

Baghdad's example also becomes stronger after correcting the story. The book need not insist that the Tigris was literally measured black to describe knowledge's fragility. Killing scholars, losing copies and destroying institutions can wound a network of learning, while other parts survive or are rebuilt. This material and human network can be traced more carefully than an unmeasured color.

What remained after the ink?

Writing changed history by allowing knowledge, rights and administration to extend beyond individual memory. It made steps reviewable, copies comparable, observations accumulable and cooperation over distance possible. It never worked alone: language, teaching, materials, readers, institutions and ethics sustained it.

The pen belongs in an account of education, not worship of a tool. Its value lies in a writer who writes justly, a reader who questions, an institution that preserves and a generation that tests and adds to its inheritance.

Frequently asked questions

Why was writing important to civilizations?

It extended memory beyond an individual, enabling accounts, laws and texts to be preserved, transported, reviewed and corrected across generations and places.

Did writing alone create civilization?

No. Institutions, education, economies, trust, media and readers matter. Writing is a powerful multiplier within that network rather than its sole cause.

Did books make the Tigris black in 1258?

Baghdad suffered destruction and loss of books and institutions. The river turning black with ink should not be treated as a literal verified measurement; it is a narrative image of catastrophe.

What does “taught by the pen” mean?

The verse affirms the blessing of teaching through writing that preserves and transmits, within a context combining reading and teaching. It does not confine knowledge to written text: the Quran also mentions expression and makes documentation a responsibility of justice.
